Chapter 19 — Culture, Religion, History & Stigma#

Most of the anxiety men carry about masturbation is not from their own experience. It’s inherited — from religious teaching, from 18th- and 19th-century medicine, from schoolyard folklore, from parents who inherited it in turn. Understanding where the shame came from is one of the most effective ways of putting it down. This chapter is a brief, descriptive history.

19.1 Antiquity: mostly indifferent#

Ancient Egypt. The god Atum was said to have created the world by masturbating — the act appears in creation mythology without apparent shame. Pharaohs reportedly masturbated ceremonially into the Nile.

Ancient Greece. Masturbation was regarded as a natural, if lowly, release — associated with slaves, satyrs, and those without partners. Diogenes the Cynic famously masturbated in the marketplace, remarking that he wished he could relieve hunger as easily by rubbing his stomach. Greek disapproval concerned excess (a lack of self-mastery), not the act itself.

Ancient Rome. Similar. Martial jokes about it; Galen recommended it for releasing retained semen, which he thought could become toxic. Roman medicine also held that excessive ejaculation weakened the body — an idea that would return with force.

Ancient China (Taoism). Semen (jing) was considered a finite vital essence whose loss depleted the body. Taoist sexual manuals advised men to have frequent sex but to retain semen — via the perineal hold-back (Chapter 12) — while bringing partners to orgasm. Masturbation to ejaculation was discouraged as waste, though arousal itself was valued. This is the historical root of today’s “semen retention” ideas.

Ancient India (Ayurveda). Semen (shukra) was similarly a concentrated essence, refined through many stages from food; its loss weakened. Dhat syndrome — anxiety about semen loss through masturbation, nocturnal emission, or urine, with fatigue and guilt — is a recognized culture-bound syndrome in South Asia, still common in clinics [Sumathipala 2004].

Judaism. The story of Onan (Genesis 38) — who “spilled his seed on the ground” rather than impregnate his dead brother’s widow and was struck down — was interpreted in the Talmudic era as condemning “wasting seed” (hotza’at zera levatalah), and the prohibition is treated seriously in Orthodox halakha. The text itself concerns Onan’s refusal of levirate duty (coitus interruptus), not masturbation; the association came later. Rabbinic literature contains strong condemnations (the Zohar calls it grave) alongside more pragmatic voices.

19.2 Christianity: from silence to sin#

The New Testament doesn’t mention masturbation. Early Church Fathers focused on lust and fornication; masturbation entered gradually.

Medieval penitentials (6th–11th centuries) list it among sexual sins with penances typically less severe than fornication. Thomas Aquinas (13th century) classified it as an “unnatural vice” — non-procreative, and therefore in his framework worse than fornication. This became the basis of Catholic teaching.

The Catholic Church today: the Catechism (§2352) calls masturbation “an intrinsically and gravely disordered action” while noting that culpability may be reduced by “affective immaturity, force of acquired habit, conditions of anxiety, or other psychological or social factors.” Pastoral practice varies widely.

Protestant traditions vary enormously. Puritan and later evangelical traditions condemned it strongly; some contemporary evangelical communities maintain this, with substantial associated distress (the moral-incongruence research, Chapter 14, is largely a study of these populations). Mainline denominations have largely moved to neutrality or acceptance.

Eastern Orthodoxy treats it as a sin requiring confession, with varying severity. Latter-day Saints have historically taught strongly against it; the tone has softened but the teaching remains.

19.3 Islam#

The Quran doesn’t mention masturbation directly; verses on guarding chastity (23:5–7, 24:30–33) are cited. Hadith are sparse and of disputed authenticity. The Sunni schools differ: Hanafi and Hanbali jurists have generally considered it forbidden but permitted as the lesser evil to avoid fornication; Maliki and most Shafi’i scholars considered it forbidden; some in each school held it merely disliked (makruh). Shia jurisprudence generally forbids it. Contemporary scholars range from prohibition to conditional permissibility. Masturbation during Ramadan fasting hours invalidates the fast in all schools.

19.4 Other traditions#

Buddhism. Monastic rules prohibit it for monks as a serious offense. For laypeople, the third precept (sexual misconduct) is generally interpreted as concerning harm to others; masturbation is not typically a violation.

Hinduism. Ranges from ascetic brahmacharya (celibacy, semen retention for spiritual power — the shukra concept) to Tantric traditions using sexual energy deliberately, to the majority of householders for whom it’s a private matter with mild disapproval at most.

Sikhism. Discouraged as lust (kaam) but not a central concern.

Indigenous and traditional cultures. Enormous variation. Anthropological surveys [Ford & Beach 1951] found some societies indifferent, some permissive, some restrictive. No universal human prohibition exists.

19.5 The medical panic: 1710–1950#

The most consequential chapter for modern attitudes is not religious but medical.

Onania (anonymous, London, c. 1712) — Onania; or, The Heinous Sin of Self Pollution, and all its Frightful Consequences — was the first mass-market work to claim masturbation caused disease: weakness, discharge, epilepsy, consumption, insanity. It sold enormously and coined “onanism.”

Samuel-Auguste Tissot, a respected Swiss physician, published L’Onanisme (1760), lending medical authority. Tissot held that semen was concentrated blood (one ounce equaled forty of blood) and that its loss — especially through masturbation, which he thought more draining than intercourse — caused weakness, memory loss, blindness, gout, tuberculosis, insanity, and death. The book was medical orthodoxy across Europe for over a century.

19th-century medicine built on Tissot. Masturbatory insanity was a standard psychiatric diagnosis; asylum records list it as a cause of admission. Physicians prescribed cold baths, bland diets, restraints, spiked rings, chastity devices, circumcision, clitoridectomy in girls, and in extreme cases cauterization or castration. Sylvester Graham (the cracker) and John Harvey Kellogg (the cereal) promoted bland diets specifically to reduce sexual excitement; Kellogg’s Plain Facts for Old and Young (1877) lists 39 signs of the “secret vice” and recommends circumcision without anesthesia for boys, “as the brief pain attending the operation will have a salutary effect upon the mind.” Boy Scout handbooks into the 1920s warned against it. Freud initially treated it as a cause of neurasthenia.

Circumcision in the anglophone world — its rise in the US, UK, Canada, and Australia in the late 19th and early 20th centuries was driven substantially by the belief that it reduced masturbation. The rationale later shifted as the masturbation theory collapsed, but the practice persisted in the US.

The collapse. By the early 20th century, evidence for Tissot’s claims was recognized as nonexistent. Havelock Ellis (Studies in the Psychology of Sex, 1897–1928) dismantled the medical case. Alfred Kinsey (1948) documented that 92% of American men masturbated — making “masturbatory insanity” statistically absurd. By the 1950s mainstream medicine had abandoned the disease model. Masters & Johnson (1966) studied it in the laboratory as normal physiology.

But the folklore — hair on the palms, blindness, weakness, “wasting” — had two centuries of head start, and it persists in playgrounds, families, and the deep background of adult male anxiety.

19.6 Modern medicine and sex education#

  • The American Medical Association declared masturbation normal in 1972.
  • The WHO, Planned Parenthood, the NHS, and virtually every mainstream medical body now describe it as a normal, healthy part of human sexuality.
  • Joycelyn Elders, US Surgeon General, was fired in 1994 for suggesting masturbation might be mentioned in sex education — the stigma retained political force into recent decades.
  • Sex therapy has used directed masturbation as standard treatment for anorgasmia, PE, and DE since the 1970s [LoPiccolo & Lobitz 1972].
  • Contemporary reversal: the 2010s saw online movements (NoFap, “semen retention,” “porn addiction” communities) revive several of Tissot’s themes — depletion, vital energy, testosterone, brain damage — in neuroscience-flavored language. Chapters 4 and 14 address the claims. The historical parallel is instructive: same anxieties, new vocabulary.

19.7 Why the stigma persists#

  1. Inherited silence. Parents don’t discuss it; schools don’t teach it; the vacuum fills with folklore.
  2. Religious teaching, for those raised in condemning traditions, creates the moral incongruence that predicts distress.
  3. Masculinity norms. Masturbation is coded as what men do when they can’t get sex. False (partnered men masturbate the most) but persistent.
  4. The secret/dirty loop. Done in secret → shame → done faster and more furtively → more shame.
  5. Commercial interests. Abstinence-coaching, supplement, and “reboot” industries profit from men believing something is wrong.
  6. Real problems generalized. Compulsive use is real for a minority (Chapter 15) and gets generalized into “masturbation is a problem.”

19.8 A pragmatic position#

You don’t need to adopt any particular view. But notice:

  • The disease claims are false — thoroughly, on the evidence.
  • Religious prohibitions are matters of faith and interpretation, not health; many religious men and their advisors find workable positions; unresolved conflict is worse for well-being than either consistent practice or consistent abstinence.
  • The “vital essence” models (Taoist, Ayurvedic, Tissot) rest on a pre-scientific understanding of semen that is simply wrong — it is continuously produced and contains no finite vital principle. The subjective experiences these traditions describe (feeling energized during abstinence) are real; the explanations aren’t.
  • Nearly all men do it; nearly all men worry about it; the worry is the inheritance, not the evidence.

Key takeaways#

  • Antiquity was mostly indifferent (Greece, Rome, Egypt) or concerned with semen-as-essence (Taoist China, Ayurvedic India). The Onan story concerns coitus interruptus, not masturbation.
  • Christianity developed prohibition gradually; Aquinas made it “unnatural.” Catholic teaching retains this; Protestant traditions vary; evangelical communities show the highest moral-incongruence distress.
  • Islam: no direct Quranic mention; schools range from forbidden to disliked to conditionally permitted.
  • The medical panic (1712–1950) — Onania, Tissot, Kellogg — invented the disease claims and drove circumcision, restraints, and bland diets. It collapsed under Ellis and Kinsey; its folklore persists.
  • Modern medicine is unanimous: normal and healthy.
  • “Semen retention” and “reboot” movements revive Tissot in new vocabulary.
  • The stigma is inherited, not evidenced.
