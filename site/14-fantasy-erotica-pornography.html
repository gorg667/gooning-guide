Chapter 14 — Fantasy, Erotica & Pornography#

Most male masturbation involves erotic material of some kind — internal fantasy, memories, written erotica, audio, images, or video. Pornography specifically is the most contested subject in this guide: one camp says it causes erectile dysfunction, addiction, and brain damage; another says the harms are moral panic with no evidence. Both are partly wrong. This chapter lays out what’s actually known, then gives practical guidance for using erotic material in a way that serves pleasure and sexual health rather than undermining them.

14.1 Fantasy#

What it is and why it works#

Sexual fantasy is deliberate or spontaneous mental imagery with erotic content. Nearly everyone has them — surveys find over 95% of men and women report sexual fantasies [Lehmiller 2018, Tell Me What You Want, survey of 4,000+ Americans]. The most common themes across genders: multi-partner sex, power/control (dominance and submission), novelty and adventure, taboo or forbidden scenarios, partner-sharing, and passion/romance.

Fantasy works because the brain’s arousal circuits respond to imagined stimuli nearly as strongly as to real ones. Imagined touch activates somatosensory areas; imagined scenarios activate the same reward and anticipation systems as real ones. Fantasy is the original erotic technology.

Fantasy vs. desire vs. behavior#

A fantasy is not a wish. Lehmiller’s data and decades of clinical experience are consistent: most people don’t want to act out most of their fantasies, and fantasizing about something says nothing reliable about what a person would do or wants to do. Fantasies about non-consent, for example, are extremely common (in women as well as men) and have no relationship to desire for actual non-consent. Fantasies about people other than one’s partner are near-universal and are not infidelity. Fantasies that conflict with one’s values or identity are common and are not evidence about one’s “true self.”

The practical point: fantasy content is not a moral or diagnostic issue. What matters is whether the material is legal and whether it’s serving you.

Using fantasy deliberately#

  • Fantasy is low-bandwidth. Unlike a screen, it leaves most of your attention available for sensation. Men who move from screen-dependence back to fantasy nearly always report richer physical sensation.
  • It’s infinitely customizable and infinitely novel. No habituation problem if you’re willing to be creative.
  • It can be aligned with reality. Perelman’s clinical work on delayed ejaculation identifies a “disparity between fantasy and reality” as a contributor — when the mental content required for arousal is far from anything a partner can provide, partnered sex suffers [Perelman 2016]. Including fantasies that resemble your actual or plausible sexual life keeps the arousal system connected to reality. This isn’t a rule against exotic fantasy; it’s a suggestion to keep the range wide.
  • It can be somatic. Instead of a visual narrative, fantasize sensation — imagine a mouth, a hand, a body, and put attention on the imagined feeling in your own body. This blends fantasy with mindful attention rather than competing with it.
  • Memory is a special case: recalling actual experiences in detail. Powerfully arousing and inherently reality-aligned.
  • Rotating fantasies prevents the same habituation that affects any stimulus.
  • Guilt about content is common and counterproductive. If the fantasy is legal and stays in your head, the guilt is the problem, not the fantasy.

14.2 Written and audio erotica#

Written erotica (stories, literature) is the oldest recorded form. It engages imagination, is low-bandwidth relative to video, has effectively infinite variety, and is text — so it requires active mental participation, which many men find produces a more engaged and less passive arousal than video.

Audio erotica (narrated stories, guided sessions, ASMR-adjacent content, recorded improvisations) has grown enormously. It lets you close your eyes — all visual cortex freed for body attention — while still providing external content. It’s a natural fit for mindful masturbation (Chapter 8). Guided audio for edging, for slow sessions, and for specific practices exists and can be genuinely useful for men learning pacing.

Both formats avoid many of the concerns raised about video pornography (below): less habituation pressure, no performer-ethics concerns with written fiction, more attention available for the body, and less “supernormal stimulus” intensity.

14.3 Pornography: the evidence#

Prevalence#

Video pornography use is near-universal among men in countries with internet access. Studies typically find 70–95% of men report use, with most young men using weekly or more [Herbenick 2020; Bőthe 2024]. It’s a normal behavior in the statistical sense. The question is not whether men use it but whether, how, and how much it causes problems.

The claims and the evidence#

Claim: Pornography causes erectile dysfunction (“PIED”).

  • Against: multiple large studies find no association between pornography use per se and erectile function [Grubbs & Gola 2019, three samples n>3,000; Landripet & Štulhofer 2015, European men]. One laboratory study found men who watched more porn had greater arousal responsiveness to sexual stimuli [Prause & Pfaus 2015]. A 2024 narrative review concluded the evidence for porn as a cause of ED is inconsistent, with some studies showing associations with better function [Rowland 2024].
  • For: consistent cross-sectional associations between problematic or compulsive pornography use (as distinct from frequency) and ED [Grubbs & Gola 2019; Jacobs 2021 n≈3,400]. Clinical case series describe young men with situational ED (fine with porn, not with a partner) that improved when they reduced pornography [Park 2016 — a much-cited but methodologically weak paper]. Rising rates of ED in young men in some surveys, temporally coinciding with high-speed internet porn — a correlation with many candidate explanations.
  • Assessment: “porn-induced ED” as a distinct medical entity is not established. The best-supported picture: for most men, porn has no effect on erectile function. For some men, a pattern — compulsive use, extreme reliance on porn for arousal, conditioning to specific stimuli, and/or the anxiety and shame that accompany self-perceived problematic use — contributes to situational difficulties. The mechanism is likely psychological (conditioning, performance anxiety, attention) more than neurological “damage.” Reversibility is generally good.

Claim: Pornography is addictive / causes “brain changes like drugs.”

  • The imaging studies cited for this (e.g., Kühn & Gallinat 2014 — smaller striatal volume in heavier users; Voon 2014 — cue reactivity in “compulsive” users similar to drug cue reactivity) are small, cross-sectional, and can’t distinguish cause from effect or from pre-existing traits. All rewarding activities produce “brain changes” — that’s what learning is. The question is whether the changes are dysfunctional.
  • The DSM-5 does not include pornography or sex addiction. The ICD-11 includes Compulsive Sexual Behaviour Disorder as an impulse control disorder, not an addiction, and its authors explicitly note that the evidence doesn’t yet support an addiction model [Kraus 2018].
  • Assessment: some men develop compulsive patterns of pornography use that meet clinical criteria for CSBD and cause real harm (Chapter 15). Whether this is “addiction” in the neurobiological sense is unresolved and partly semantic. The behavioral reality — difficulty controlling use despite negative consequences — is real for an estimated 3–10% of users, and the language matters less than the help.

Claim: Pornography causes escalation to more extreme content.

  • Habituation to any stimulus is real (Chapter 3). Some users report seeking progressively more novel or intense content. Survey data find that a minority report this pattern; most users’ preferences are stable over time [e.g., Bőthe 2021]. Escalation is more associated with problematic use than with use per se.
  • Assessment: escalation is a real pattern for some users, driven by the novelty-seeking of the dopamine system, and is a reasonable thing to monitor in oneself. It is not inevitable.

Claim: Pornography harms relationships.

  • Mixed. Some studies find associations between solo porn use and lower relationship/sexual satisfaction, especially when use is secretive or when partners disapprove; other studies find no effect or positive effects when use is shared or open [Kohut 2017; Willoughby 2016]. Effects are heavily moderated by relationship context, communication, and moral attitudes.
  • Assessment: secrecy and value conflict cause more harm than use. Open communication about porn in a relationship is protective.

Claim: Pornography shapes sexual expectations and scripts unrealistically.

  • Well-supported for adolescents and for men with little partnered experience. Porn depicts sex that is performative, non-representative in bodies, duration, acts, and responses. Men who use it as a sexual education source hold more distorted expectations [Rothman 2021 and others].
  • Assessment: true and important, especially for younger men. Porn is fiction; treating it as documentary is the error.

Claim: “Porn is harmless.”

  • Overstated in the other direction. The evidence shows most use is not associated with measurable harm; it doesn’t show that no use is ever harmful. Compulsive patterns, conditioning effects, attention displacement, expectation distortion, and ethical concerns about production are all real for a subset of users and content.

The “supernormal stimulus” argument#

A more nuanced version of the concern, independent of “addiction”: internet pornography provides unlimited novelty (infinite partners, infinite scenarios) with zero effort, at intensity levels (visual, auditory, editing) that exceed anything in real sexual life. The dopamine system responds to novelty and prediction of reward; a stimulus engineered for maximal novelty is a supernormal stimulus — like junk food is to hunger. The concern is not damage but comparison: real partners and real sensation compete with an artificially intensified reference point.

This argument is plausible and consistent with basic neuroscience; it’s also hard to test directly. Its practical implication is the same as the conditioning argument: variety in the type of stimulus, including stimulus-free sessions, keeps the reference point realistic.

The moral-incongruence finding#

One of the most robust findings in this literature: the distress associated with pornography use is largely predicted by the gap between a person’s use and their moral beliefs about it, not by the amount of use [Grubbs 2019, “Pornography Problems Due to Moral Incongruence” — meta-analytic review]. Religious men who use porn report far more “addiction” and distress at the same or lower frequencies than non-religious men. This doesn’t mean their distress is fake — it means the problem is often the conflict, not the behavior, and that resolving it may involve examining the belief as much as changing the behavior. ICD-11 explicitly excludes moral-conflict distress from the CSBD diagnosis for this reason.

14.4 Practical guidance#

Given the above, here’s what a reasonable man should do.

Principles#

  1. Porn is optional. Many men use it, many don’t, both are fine. It is not necessary for good masturbation, and Chapters 8 and 11 argue that screen-free sessions are generally more pleasurable once you adapt.
  2. Use, don’t be used. The design of pornography platforms (infinite scroll, autoplay, algorithmic recommendation) is optimized for time-on-site, not for your pleasure or well-being. Choosing content deliberately and then stopping browsing is different from letting the platform drive.
  3. Attention is the cost. Every minute watching is a minute not feeling. The trade is sometimes worth it; it shouldn’t be the default for the whole session.
  4. Keep the range wide. Screen sessions, fantasy sessions, audio sessions, eyes-closed sessions, mindful sessions. If you can’t get aroused or reach orgasm without a screen, the range has narrowed — widen it.
  5. Watch for the pattern, not the number. Compulsive use looks like: using when you don’t want to; escalating time or intensity; failing to stop when you intend to; using to avoid feelings; continuing despite consequences; distress. Frequency alone doesn’t define it (Chapter 15).
  6. Porn is fiction. Bodies, durations, acts, responses, and dynamics are performed. It teaches nothing reliable about real sex.

Techniques for hybrid use#

  • Front-load. Use material to get to arousal 6–7, then close it, close your eyes, and spend the plateau and orgasm on sensation. Most men who try this find the orgasm markedly stronger.
  • Alternate. One minute watching, two minutes eyes closed. The material becomes a periodic input rather than a continuous one.
  • Audio only. Listen; don’t watch. Eyes closed throughout.
  • Single piece, no browsing. Choose one thing beforehand. Watch it. Don’t open a second tab. Browsing is the compulsive mechanic — the search for the “perfect” clip is a novelty-seeking loop that can consume the entire session and dilute arousal.
  • Still images or written content over video: lower intensity, more imagination, more attention left over.
  • Schedule screen-free sessions — at least one in three, or one week per month. Not as penance; as range maintenance.
  • No porn during edging plateau. Use it for the build if you like; then put it away. The plateau is for the body.
  • Never porn as the “finishing move” every time. If orgasm always coincides with a screen, the screen becomes the conditioned trigger.

If you think you have a problem#

Signs worth taking seriously:

  • You’ve tried to cut back and couldn’t.
  • Use has escalated in time, frequency, or content intensity, and you’re not comfortable with the trajectory.
  • You need porn to get or stay aroused, including with a partner.
  • You’re using it to manage emotions (loneliness, anxiety, boredom) more than for pleasure.
  • It’s displacing sleep, work, social life, or partnered sex.
  • You feel worse afterward, consistently.
  • You’re hiding the extent of it.

If several apply: Chapter 15 covers self-assessment and strategies. Briefly: a structured break (2–6 weeks) from pornography specifically — not necessarily from masturbation — is the most commonly effective first step. During the break, masturbate with fantasy and attention to sensation. Most men find that arousal to non-screen stimuli returns within 2–4 weeks and that the break recalibrates their relationship to the material. If the break isn’t possible or doesn’t help, a sex therapist (AASECT-certified or equivalent) is the right resource; porn-problem-specific programs vary widely in quality and some are religiously motivated in ways that add shame — choose carefully.

A note on “reboots” and NoFap. Extended abstinence from both porn and masturbation is the approach of large online communities. The evidence: no physiological benefits from abstinence (Chapter 4); the psychological benefits reported by many participants are real experiences that most likely come from breaking a compulsive pattern, community support, and redirected attention. The costs: rigid, all-or-nothing frameworks; shame on “relapse”; unsupported pseudoscience (the testosterone claims, “brain rewiring” timelines like “90 days”); and, in some communities, misogyny and moral panic. If a structured break helps you, take one — but a break from porn and compulsive patterns is what most men actually need, and masturbation itself isn’t the problem.

14.5 Ethics of content#

This guide is about health and pleasure, but content choices have ethical dimensions worth naming:

  • Legality. Content involving minors is illegal everywhere and causes direct harm. Content depicting real non-consent is illegal in most places. This is absolute.
  • Consent of performers. Professional studio content generally involves consenting adult performers with contracts; amateur content uploaded without consent (“revenge porn,” leaked content, hidden cameras) does not. The proliferation of non-consensual content on free tube sites is well-documented. Paying for content from performers or studios that verify consent, or using platforms with strong verification, addresses this.
  • Labor conditions. Vary enormously. Performer-owned platforms and content have grown and give more control to the people in it.
  • AI-generated content. Emerging ethical and legal issues around likeness, consent, and training data. Deepfakes of real people without consent are harmful and increasingly illegal.
  • Written fiction and audio involving only adult creators have the fewest ethical concerns.

Chapter 20 covers legal and privacy aspects.

Key takeaways#

  • Fantasy is near-universal, isn’t a wish or a diagnosis, is low-bandwidth (leaves attention for sensation), infinitely novel, and can be kept reality-aligned. It’s the original and often best erotic technology.
  • Written and audio erotica engage imagination, leave the eyes closed, and avoid most concerns raised about video.
  • Pornography and ED: no association for use per se; consistent association for problematic/compulsive use. “PIED” as a distinct disorder isn’t established; conditioning and anxiety are the likely mechanisms; reversible.
  • “Addiction”: unresolved and partly semantic. Compulsive use meeting CSBD criteria is real for an estimated 3–10%. Moral incongruence predicts distress better than frequency.
  • Porn is fiction and a poor sex educator. Escalation is a real but not inevitable pattern.
  • Practical: front-load material then close your eyes; no browsing; audio over video; schedule screen-free sessions; never porn as the exclusive finishing trigger.
  • If use feels problematic: a 2–6 week break from porn (not necessarily masturbation) usually recalibrates. Beyond that, a certified sex therapist.
  • Ethics: legal, consenting adult performers; be aware that free tube sites host non-consensual content; fiction and audio have the fewest concerns.
