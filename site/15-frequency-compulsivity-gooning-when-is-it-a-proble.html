Chapter 15 — Frequency, Compulsivity & “Gooning”: When Is It a Problem?#

This guide has spent fourteen chapters teaching you to masturbate more skillfully, more pleasurably, and for longer. This chapter is the counterweight: how to know if your relationship with masturbation has become a problem, what the clinical criteria actually are, what “gooning” is and where it sits, and what to do if you need to change something. It’s written without moralizing, because moralizing is the main obstacle to men getting accurate information about this.

15.1 Frequency is the wrong metric#

Every man wonders at some point whether he does it “too much.” The answer is that the number doesn’t tell you.

Population data (Chapter 1, 4): men masturbate anywhere from never to several times daily; the middle of the distribution for men under 40 is a few times per week; daily is common; multiple times daily is not rare in younger men. No frequency has been associated with harm in the absence of other problems.

A man who masturbates twice a day, enjoys it, sleeps well, works, has relationships, and can skip it without distress when circumstances require does not have a problem. A man who masturbates three times a week but does it only to numb anxiety, feels terrible afterward, has tried to stop and can’t, and is avoiding a partner because of it might.

Replace “how much” with these questions:

  1. Control. Can I choose not to when I decide not to? When I intend to stop, do I stop?
  2. Interference. Is it costing me sleep, work, study, relationships, money, or health?
  3. Function. Is it displacing partnered sex I’d otherwise want, or making partnered sex harder?
  4. Motive. Am I doing it primarily for pleasure — or primarily to escape, numb, procrastinate, or manage a mood?
  5. Enjoyment. Is it still good? Or has it become mechanical, joyless, something I do on autopilot?
  6. Escalation. Is the time, frequency, or intensity of content increasing in a way I’m not comfortable with?
  7. Aftermath. How do I feel afterward, consistently? Relaxed and content — or empty, ashamed, anxious?
  8. Physical. Pain, irritation, numbness, exhaustion?

Green on all eight: no problem, whatever the number. Red on several: worth attention, whatever the number.

15.2 Compulsive Sexual Behaviour Disorder: the clinical picture#

In 2019, the World Health Organization included Compulsive Sexual Behaviour Disorder (CSBD, code 6C72) in ICD-11 under impulse control disorders. It is the only formal diagnostic category for “out of control” sexual behavior in a major classification system (the DSM-5 declined to include “hypersexual disorder”). The criteria, paraphrased [Kraus 2018; WHO ICD-11]:

A persistent pattern of failure to control intense, repetitive sexual impulses or urges, resulting in repetitive sexual behaviour, manifested in one or more of: - Sexual activity becomes a central focus of life to the point of neglecting health, personal care, or other interests, activities, and responsibilities. - Numerous unsuccessful efforts to significantly reduce the behaviour. - Continued behaviour despite adverse consequences or deriving little or no satisfaction from it. - Continued behaviour even when the person derives little or no satisfaction from it.

The pattern is manifested over an extended period (e.g., 6 months or more) and causes marked distress or significant impairment in personal, family, social, educational, occupational, or other important areas of functioning.

Distress that is entirely related to moral judgments and disapproval about sexual impulses, urges, or behaviours is not sufficient to meet this requirement.

Several things follow:

  • It’s about control and consequence, not amount. High frequency without loss of control or harm is not CSBD.
  • Moral distress alone doesn’t count. A religious man who masturbates weekly and feels sinful about it does not have CSBD. His distress is real and deserves attention — but the clinical category is designed to exclude it, because the “problem” is the conflict between behavior and belief, and treating it as a disorder of the behavior is a category error [Grubbs 2019; Gola 2020].
  • It’s an impulse control disorder, not an addiction. The WHO working group considered and declined the addiction framing on evidence grounds. This matters for treatment: addiction models emphasize abstinence and powerlessness; impulse-control models emphasize learning regulation. The latter is more consistent with the evidence and with most successful treatment.
  • Prevalence estimates run 3–10% of adults depending on criteria and population, higher in men than women [Bőthe 2024; Dickenson 2018]. It’s not rare, and it’s not most men.
  • Masturbation and pornography are the most common behaviors involved, but CSBD can involve any sexual behavior.
  • Comorbidity is high: depression, anxiety, ADHD, OCD, trauma history, and substance use disorders are common alongside. Often the sexual behavior is downstream of these — a regulation strategy for something else.

15.3 “Gooning”#

The term. “Gooning” (from “goon,” a slang term for a trance-like or mindless state) refers to prolonged masturbation sessions — often hours — characterized by extended edging, continuous consumption of pornography, and a deliberately sought dissociative, trance-like state of high arousal (“goon state” or “gooned out”). The associated online communities and content often feature escalating, sometimes deliberately degrading self-talk, “porn addiction” framed as a fetish, and an identity built around the practice.

What’s actually happening physiologically. Extended edging at 8–9 with continuous novel visual stimuli produces sustained high dopamine (anticipation) with delayed or absent consummation (orgasm). The “trance” is the narrowed attention and mild prefrontal deactivation of high arousal (Chapter 3), extended for hours, combined with the hypnotic effect of continuous scrolling through novel content. The self-degrading content is a form of arousal amplification through taboo/shame. None of this is neurologically exotic; it’s the wanting system run at maximum for a very long time.

Is it a problem? It depends entirely on the 15.1 questions.

  • A man who, once a month, sets aside an evening for a three-hour edging session with pornography, enjoys it, has an intense orgasm, and goes about his life — is doing an intense version of the practices in Chapters 9 and 11. Not a problem.
  • A man who does this most nights, into the early hours, skipping sleep; who can’t have a session under two hours; who finds ordinary orgasms unsatisfying; who has adopted an identity (“gooner”) organized around it; who has escalated content; who feels wrecked afterward; who has tried to cut back and failed — has a pattern that maps closely onto CSBD, whatever he calls it.

The specific risks of the gooning pattern, as distinct from ordinary long sessions:

  1. Time. Multi-hour sessions, regularly, consume a life.
  2. Sleep. The pattern is overwhelmingly nocturnal. Chronic sleep loss is a serious health problem on its own and worsens impulse control (a vicious cycle).
  3. Conditioning to extremes. Hours of edging with continuous novel content sets the arousal reference point at a level no partner, and no ordinary session, can match. This is the supernormal-stimulus concern (Chapter 14) at its most concentrated.
  4. Attention displacement. Gooning is almost entirely screen-focused. The body is barely felt. It’s the opposite of the mindful approach — arousal without sensation. Men who goon heavily often report that they can no longer enjoy a “normal” session.
  5. Identity. Building an identity around a sexual behavior — especially one framed in terms of addiction and degradation — is psychologically sticky. It converts a behavior into a self-concept, which makes change feel like self-betrayal.
  6. The content culture. The self-degrading, “porn addict as fetish” content that accompanies gooning eroticizes loss of control. This is a direct obstacle to regaining it: the shame becomes part of the arousal loop.
  7. Physical. Hours of stimulation without lube breaks, dehydration, prostate/testicular congestion, wrist strain, posture.

The honest assessment: the gooning practice (long edging with pornography) is an intensity setting that some men can use occasionally without harm. The gooning culture and pattern (nightly, escalating, identity-forming, degradation-oriented) is a description of compulsive sexual behavior with a subculture built around it. The distinguishing factor is not the length of the session; it’s control, consequence, and the trajectory.

If you recognize yourself in the second description and don’t like it, section 15.5 is for you. If you recognize yourself and are fine with it, the 15.1 questions are still worth answering honestly.

15.4 Self-assessment#

Answer honestly, thinking about the past six months.

Part A — Control 1. I’ve tried to reduce or stop and haven’t been able to. (Y/N) 2. I often masturbate for longer than I intended. (Y/N) 3. I often masturbate when I’ve decided not to. (Y/N) 4. I’ve broken commitments to myself about it repeatedly. (Y/N)

Part B — Consequences 5. It regularly costs me sleep. (Y/N) 6. It’s affected my work or study. (Y/N) 7. It’s affected a relationship (partner, family, friends). (Y/N) 8. I’ve had physical problems from it (pain, irritation, injury). (Y/N) 9. I’ve spent money I couldn’t afford on it. (Y/N)

Part C — Function 10. I have difficulty getting aroused or reaching orgasm without pornography. (Y/N) 11. I have difficulty getting aroused or reaching orgasm with a partner but not alone. (Y/N) 12. I’ve avoided partnered sex or intimacy because of masturbation/porn use. (Y/N)

Part D — Motive and aftermath 13. I mainly do it to cope with negative feelings (stress, loneliness, boredom, anxiety) rather than for pleasure. (Y/N) 14. I usually feel bad afterward (empty, ashamed, anxious, depressed). (Y/N) 15. It’s become mechanical — I do it without really enjoying it. (Y/N)

Part E — Escalation and preoccupation 16. The time I spend has increased substantially. (Y/N) 17. The content I need has become more extreme than I’m comfortable with. (Y/N) 18. I think about it a lot when I’m not doing it. (Y/N) 19. I’ve organized my schedule or life around it. (Y/N)

Part F — The moral check 20. If I imagine that masturbation were completely morally neutral — as neutral as eating lunch — would I still be concerned about my pattern? (Y/N)

Interpretation:

  • 0–3 Y in A–E: Normal range. Whatever your frequency.
  • 4–7 Y, spread across sections: Worth attention. Not necessarily a disorder; a pattern that would benefit from the strategies in 15.5. Consider talking to someone.
  • 8+ Y, or 3+ in Part A, or 2+ in Part C: Consistent with CSBD or a significant problematic pattern. A professional consultation is a good idea.
  • Part F = N with several Y elsewhere: Your concern may be primarily moral incongruence. This is worth working on — with a therapist, a thoughtful religious advisor, or through reading — but the intervention is on the belief-behavior conflict, not necessarily on the behavior. Shame-based abstinence programs often make this worse.

This is not a validated instrument. Validated ones exist (the Compulsive Sexual Behavior Disorder Scale [CSBD-19, Bőthe 2020]; the Problematic Pornography Consumption Scale [PPCS]; the Hypersexual Behavior Inventory [HBI]) and a clinician can administer them.

15.5 Changing the pattern#

If you’ve decided you want to change — not because someone told you to, not because of shame, but because your honest answers say the pattern is costing you — here is what works, roughly in order of evidence and practicality.

Reframe#

  • You are not an addict; you have a habit with a strong reward loop. The impulse-control frame is more accurate and more useful. Habits are changed by changing cues, routines, and rewards.
  • Masturbation is not the enemy. For most men with a problematic pattern, the problem is a specific configuration — usually late-night + screen + browsing + hours + isolation. Change the configuration; keep the masturbation.
  • The goal is agency, not abstinence. You’re aiming to masturbate when you choose, how you choose, for as long as you choose, and to enjoy it. That’s the mastery this guide is about. Abstinence is one tool, not the goal.
  • Shame is fuel for the loop. Feeling terrible → needing relief → masturbating → feeling terrible. Breaking the shame link breaks the loop. Chapter 4’s facts help. So does saying it out loud to someone who won’t judge.

Change the configuration#

The most effective interventions change how rather than whether:

  • No screens. For a defined period (start with 2–4 weeks), masturbate only with fantasy or audio. This is the single highest-yield change for most men with a problematic pattern, because the screen is where the browsing loop, the time loss, the escalation, and the attention displacement all live. You’ll likely find arousal harder at first and then find it comes back with more sensation (Chapter 8, 14).
  • Time limits. Decide the length before you start. Set a timer. 15 minutes, or 30. When it goes off, finish or stop. This restores the experience of choosing.
  • Not in bed / not at night. Move sessions to a different time and place. The late-night bed is the strongest cue for the compulsive pattern. Morning or afternoon, in a chair or on the floor, changes the context and breaks the cue.
  • Never as a response to a feeling. When you notice the urge, name the feeling underneath it (bored? anxious? lonely? avoiding something?). Address that feeling directly, even briefly. Then, if you still want to masturbate, do it — as a choice, not a reflex.
  • Intentional, planned sessions. Decide in advance: “Wednesday evening, 30 minutes, no screen, full attention.” Then keep the appointment. Planned pleasure is the opposite of compulsive pleasure.
  • Mindful practice. Chapter 8. Compulsive sessions are dissociated — attention elsewhere, body barely felt. Mindful sessions are the opposite. Retraining attention onto sensation is directly therapeutic.
  • Friction on the platform. Block or uninstall the specific sites/apps. Use a blocker with a delay. Remove saved logins. This isn’t about willpower — it’s about making the compulsive path slower than the deliberate one.

Address what’s underneath#

Compulsive masturbation is almost always doing a job. Common ones:

  • Anxiety regulation. Orgasm is a reliable, fast anxiolytic. If it’s your main one, you need others: exercise, breath work, actual treatment for anxiety.
  • Loneliness. Porn simulates connection. It’s a poor substitute and it can reduce motivation to seek the real thing.
  • Procrastination / avoidance. Masturbation is the perfect procrastination — absorbing, rewarding, and it “resets” the mind. If sessions cluster around deadlines or difficult tasks, that’s the job it’s doing.
  • Sleep avoidance / insomnia. Late-night sessions can be a way of not confronting the difficulty of sleep, or of not ending the day.
  • Depression. Anhedonia + a reliable pleasure source = heavy reliance.
  • ADHD. Impulsivity, dopamine-seeking, and difficulty with delayed gratification all predispose to compulsive patterns. Untreated ADHD is heavily overrepresented in CSBD populations.
  • Trauma. Sexual compulsivity is common in trauma survivors. Dissociative sexual states can be a re-enactment or a coping mechanism.
  • Sexual frustration in a relationship. Desire discrepancy that isn’t being discussed.

Treating the underlying thing is usually more effective than fighting the symptom. If you recognize one of these, it’s the place to focus.

Structured breaks#

A defined break — 2–6 weeks — from pornography specifically (not necessarily from masturbation) is the most commonly useful reset:

  • It breaks the cue-routine loop.
  • It recalibrates arousal to non-screen stimuli.
  • It creates a period in which you find out what the behavior was doing for you (the feelings that surface when it’s unavailable are the information).
  • It’s finite, so it’s not an identity (“I don’t watch porn” vs. “I’m a recovering porn addict”).

Rules for a good break: pick a defined end date; decide in advance what’s allowed (masturbation with fantasy? audio? nothing?); tell one person; expect the first week to be the hardest; treat a lapse as data, not failure (“what was the cue?”) and continue; at the end, decide deliberately what your relationship to the material will be going forward.

A break from all masturbation is a different and larger intervention, useful mainly if masturbation itself (not just porn) has become compulsive, or if you want to reset sensitivity after heavy use. The physiological benefits claimed by abstinence communities aren’t real (Chapter 4); the psychological ones can be.

Professional help#

When to seek it:

  • The self-assessment scores high.
  • You’ve tried the above for a couple of months and the pattern persists.
  • There’s a co-occurring issue (depression, anxiety, ADHD, trauma, substance use).
  • It’s affecting a relationship significantly.
  • You feel hopeless about it.

Who:

  • A certified sex therapist (AASECT in the US, COSRT in the UK, or equivalent). They treat CSBD without moralizing and understand the difference between compulsivity and moral incongruence. This is the best first choice.
  • A psychologist or psychiatrist with experience in impulse control or behavioral addictions, especially if there’s comorbidity.
  • Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) and acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) have the best evidence base for CSBD. Mindfulness-based approaches also show promise.
  • Medication is sometimes used — SSRIs (which also reduce libido), naltrexone (opioid antagonist, some evidence for compulsive sexual behavior), or treating underlying ADHD/depression. Prescribed by a psychiatrist.
  • 12-step groups (SAA, SLAA, SA) help some men through structure and community. Caveats: the addiction/powerlessness model isn’t well-supported by evidence; some groups have moral or religious framings that add shame; SA in particular defines any sex outside marriage as acting out. Choose a group whose framework fits your values.
  • Online “porn addiction recovery” programs: hugely variable. Some are reasonable; many are religiously motivated, pseudoscientific (“rewiring in 90 days”), shame-heavy, or expensive coaching without credentials. Evaluate carefully.

What to avoid: anyone who tells you masturbation itself is the disease; anyone who promises a fixed-day cure; anyone who explains your problem primarily in terms of testosterone, “dopamine receptors,” or “brain damage”; anyone selling supplements.

15.6 The other direction: too little, or shame-driven avoidance#

Less discussed: men who avoid masturbation out of shame, religious guilt, or misinformation, and who experience:

  • Unwanted nocturnal emissions (normal, but distressing to some).
  • Difficulty with partnered sex from lack of self-knowledge (not knowing what works, poor ejaculatory control from never practicing).
  • Sexual anxiety generally.
  • A cycle of suppression → intense urge → “giving in” → shame → suppression, which mimics compulsivity but is actually driven by restriction.

For these men, the intervention is the opposite: permission, accurate information (Chapter 4), and deliberate, guilt-free practice. The restriction is the problem. Moral incongruence work applies here too.

15.7 Relationship contexts#

  • Masturbation in a relationship is normal and doesn’t indicate dissatisfaction. Most partnered men masturbate. Desire discrepancy — one partner wanting sex more often — is the most common sexual complaint in couples, and solo sex is the healthiest way to manage it.
  • Secrecy is the problem, not the behavior. Partners who feel deceived about porn or masturbation react to the deception. Open, non-defensive conversation (“I masturbate; here’s roughly how often; here’s what I use; it’s not about you”) usually defuses it.
  • When it displaces partnered sex you’d want, or when a partner is sexually available and you choose solo instead consistently, that’s information about the relationship, the pattern, or both.
  • Partners who demand total abstinence from masturbation are making an unusual and often unhealthy demand. Negotiation about porn is common and reasonable; prohibition of solo sex is a different thing.

15.8 A closing note#

The skills in this guide — edging, pleasure maximization, long sessions, toys, deliberate use of erotic material — are dual-use. They can make solo sex extraordinary and they can, in a different configuration, feed a compulsive pattern. The difference is not the technique. It’s whether you’re driving.

The test is simple and worth running periodically: Can I skip it easily when I choose to? Do I enjoy it when I do it? Is my life better for it? Yes, yes, yes: you’re fine, and you should enjoy it. Anything else: worth a look, without shame.

Key takeaways#

  • Frequency is not the metric. Control, interference, function, motive, enjoyment, escalation, aftermath, and physical symptoms are.
  • CSBD (ICD-11): failure to control + neglect of life / repeated failed attempts to cut down / continuing despite harm or lack of satisfaction, for 6+ months, with real impairment. Moral distress alone doesn’t qualify. ~3–10% of adults. Impulse control, not addiction.
  • “Gooning”: the practice (long edging + porn) is an intensity setting some men can use occasionally. The pattern and culture (nightly, escalating, identity-forming, degradation-eroticizing, sleep-destroying) describes compulsive behavior. Length isn’t the issue; control and trajectory are.
  • Self-assess with the 20 questions. Note Part F — the moral check.
  • To change: reframe (habit, not addiction; masturbation isn’t the enemy; goal is agency); change the configuration (no screens, time limits, not in bed at night, planned sessions, mindful practice, platform friction); address what’s underneath (anxiety, loneliness, avoidance, sleep, depression, ADHD, trauma, relationship); structured 2–6 week porn break; certified sex therapist if needed.
  • Avoid shame-based, pseudoscientific, or moralizing programs.
  • Restriction and shame create their own dysfunction; permission and information fix it.
  • Periodic test: Can I skip it easily? Do I enjoy it? Is my life better for it?
