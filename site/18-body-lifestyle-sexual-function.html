Chapter 18 — Body, Lifestyle & Sexual Function#

Sexual function is a whole-body output. Erection is cardiovascular; sensation is neurological; desire is hormonal and psychological; stamina is muscular and respiratory. This chapter covers what actually moves the needle — exercise, sleep, weight, diet, substances — and, equally important, what doesn’t (most supplements). Evidence ratings throughout.

18.1 Exercise — 🟢 the strongest lever#

Aerobic exercise improves erectile function. A meta-analysis of randomized trials found that moderate-to-vigorous aerobic exercise (about 40 minutes, 4 times per week, for 6 months) significantly improved erectile function scores in men with ED from vascular, metabolic, or lifestyle causes [Gerbild 2018]. The effect size approaches that of PDE5 inhibitors in some analyses [Khera 2023 meta-analysis]. Mechanism: endothelial function, nitric oxide availability, blood pressure, weight, insulin sensitivity — all the things erection depends on.

Resistance training supports testosterone (modestly), body composition, and confidence. Not directly studied for erectile function as much as aerobic, but complementary.

Pelvic floor training — Chapter 10. Its own category of exercise with direct sexual effects.

Flexibility and hip mobility — relaxed hips = relaxed pelvic floor. Yoga has small studies showing improved sexual function in men [Dhikav 2010].

Timing relative to sessions. 30–90 minutes after moderate exercise: elevated blood flow, endorphins, often heightened sensation and arousal (Chapter 11). Immediately after exhaustive exercise: worse. Sessions during a rest day: fine.

Cycling. Long hours on a narrow saddle compress the perineum and pudendal nerve — associated with numbness and ED in heavy cyclists. Mitigations: cut-out or noseless saddle, standing regularly, proper fit, padded shorts. Recreational cycling is fine.

Overtraining — chronic excessive endurance training without recovery lowers testosterone and libido. Rare in recreational exercisers.

18.2 Sleep — 🟢#

  • Testosterone is produced mainly during sleep, especially during the first REM cycles. A week of 5-hour sleep reduced daytime testosterone by 10–15% in healthy young men [Leproult 2011].
  • Nocturnal erections occur during REM; they’re part of penile tissue maintenance.
  • Sleep apnea is strongly associated with ED (via hypoxia, fragmented sleep, and reduced testosterone); treating it improves erectile function. Snoring + daytime fatigue + ED = get a sleep study.
  • Sleep deprivation reduces libido, erection quality, sensation, and impulse control (relevant to compulsivity, Chapter 15).
  • Masturbation before sleep is associated with faster sleep onset and better perceived sleep quality [Lastella 2019]. Reasonable as a sleep aid, if it doesn’t become a compulsive pre-sleep ritual.
  • Late-night sessions that cut into sleep are the worst trade in this guide: the session is low-quality (fatigue) and the sleep loss degrades everything.

18.3 Weight and metabolic health — 🟢#

  • Obesity is associated with ED (2–3× risk), lower testosterone (aromatization of T to estrogen in fat tissue; also lower SHBG dynamics), lower libido, and reduced sensation (neuropathy in diabetes).
  • Weight loss improves erectile function and testosterone in obese men — in one trial, a third of obese men with ED regained normal function through diet and exercise alone [Esposito 2004].
  • Diabetes is a major cause of ED (via vascular and nerve damage) and reduced sensation. Glucose control preserves function.
  • Metabolic syndrome (abdominal obesity, high BP, high triglycerides, low HDL, insulin resistance) is the cluster most predictive of ED.
  • Blood pressure: hypertension damages the arteries; many antihypertensives (beta-blockers, thiazides) impair erection; others (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, some calcium channel blockers) are neutral or slightly positive. Discuss with the prescriber if erections suffered after starting a medication.
  • Cholesterol: atherosclerosis in penile arteries precedes coronary disease. Statins have mixed effects — improving erectile function in some men via vascular health, with occasional reports of the opposite.

18.4 Diet — 🟡#

What has evidence:

  • Mediterranean-pattern diets (vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, fish, olive oil, nuts; low processed food) are associated with lower ED incidence and improved erectile function in trials [Esposito 2006; Bauer 2020 — Health Professionals Follow-up Study]. Mechanism: vascular health.
  • Flavonoid-rich foods (berries, citrus, apples, red wine in moderation) were associated with reduced ED incidence in a large cohort [Cassidy 2016].
  • Nitrate-rich vegetables (beets, leafy greens) support nitric oxide production — mechanistically relevant; direct erectile trials are small.
  • Hydration affects ejaculate volume and general function. Not complicated.
  • Zinc: deficiency impairs testosterone and sperm; supplementation helps only if deficient. Most men in developed countries aren’t.

What doesn’t have evidence (despite marketing):

  • “Semen volume” foods (celery, pineapple, bananas, oysters): no controlled evidence for volume. Pineapple/citrus and reduced red meat/garlic/asparagus may modestly affect taste/smell — relevant to partners, not solo. Hydration and abstinence duration are the volume levers.
  • “Aphrodisiac” foods: oysters (zinc — only helps if deficient), chocolate (mild mood effect), chili (capsaicin — mild endorphin release), watermelon (citrulline — trace amounts, far below supplement doses): placebo-range effects at best.
  • “Testosterone-boosting” foods: no food meaningfully raises testosterone in men who aren’t deficient in a specific nutrient. Adequate total calories, adequate fat (very low-fat diets lower T slightly), and adequate protein are the diet inputs; beyond that, it’s body fat and sleep.

Alcohol — see 18.6.

The men’s sexual supplement industry is enormous and largely unsupported. What the evidence says:

Bottom line: if you have mild ED, L-citrulline or L-arginine + pycnogenol are the only supplements with reasonable evidence, and prescription PDE5 inhibitors work far better. If you’re deficient in zinc, D, or magnesium, correct it. Nothing over the counter meaningfully raises testosterone in a healthy man. Avoid “enhancement” blends — they’re frequently adulterated and the adulterants are the active ingredient.

18.6 Substances#

Alcohol — 🟢 well-characterized#

  • Acute: one drink may reduce anxiety and inhibition; beyond that, alcohol impairs erection (it’s a CNS depressant and vasodilator that also reduces the erectile response), delays or prevents orgasm, reduces sensation, and impairs judgment. “Whiskey dick” is real and dose-dependent.
  • Chronic heavy use: lowers testosterone, damages nerves (neuropathy), damages the liver (which affects hormone metabolism), and causes persistent ED and reduced libido. Reversible in early stages.
  • For solo sex: a drink is fine; more than one degrades the experience.

Tobacco/nicotine — 🟢#

  • The single most damaging common substance for erections. Nicotine constricts blood vessels acutely (an immediate effect on erection quality) and smoking causes long-term vascular damage. Smokers have roughly double the ED risk; quitting improves erectile function within months [Pourmand 2004].
  • Vaping delivers nicotine and has the acute vasoconstrictive effect; the long-term vascular effect is less studied but not zero.

Cannabis — 🟡 mixed#

  • Low doses: many men report enhanced sensation, time dilation (sessions feel longer), heightened attention to the body, and increased orgasm intensity. Survey data support this for a majority of users [Sun & Eisenberg 2017 found more frequent sex among users; Wiebe 2019 — subjective enhancement].
  • Higher doses: distraction, anxiety, erectile softening (THC has vasoactive effects), difficulty reaching orgasm, sedation.
  • Chronic heavy use: associated with lower libido and possibly reduced testosterone in some studies, inconsistent.
  • Practical: if legal where you are and you use it, low doses (a small amount, or low-THC/higher-CBD) taken 30–60 minutes before a session enhance the experience for many men. High doses don’t. Edibles’ delayed onset makes dosing harder. Not a recommendation to start; information for those who use.

Caffeine — 🟡#

  • Moderate intake (1–3 cups/day) is associated with lower ED risk in observational data [Lopez 2015], possibly via vascular effects.
  • Large doses close to a session raise sympathetic tone (faster ejaculation, more anxiety, less relaxation). A coffee an hour before a slow session is counterproductive for many men.

Poppers (alkyl nitrites) — 🟠 with real risks#

  • Smooth muscle relaxant; brief rush, warmth, anal sphincter relaxation, heightened sensation for 1–3 minutes. Used in gay sexual culture and increasingly beyond it.
  • Risks: sudden blood pressure drop (dangerous with PDE5 inhibitors — potentially fatal hypotension), headache, dizziness, erectile softening (vasodilation), chemical burns if spilled on skin, methemoglobinemia with ingestion, and a specific form of retinal damage (maculopathy) linked to isopropyl nitrite formulations. Legal status varies.
  • Not recommended. If used: never with ED drugs; never ingested; sitting or lying down.

Stimulants (cocaine, methamphetamine, amphetamines, MDMA) — 🔴#

  • Intense wanting, impaired liking. Massively increased libido and compulsivity with impaired erection (vasoconstriction — “crystal dick”), delayed or absent orgasm, and prolonged, dissociated, sometimes injurious sessions. Methamphetamine in particular is associated with compulsive sexual behavior, hours-long sessions, tissue injury from lack of sensation, and high-risk behavior. MDMA impairs erection and orgasm in most men despite increased sensuality.
  • Prescription stimulants (ADHD medication) at therapeutic doses: variable; some men report reduced libido or ED, others no effect. Talk to the prescriber.

Opioids — 🔴#

  • Chronic use lowers testosterone (opioid-induced androgen deficiency), reduces libido, impairs orgasm. Acute use impairs orgasm and erection.

Psychedelics — 🟠#

  • Not generally used for sex; effects are unpredictable; sensory enhancement is possible but attention is typically elsewhere. Not recommended in this context.

18.7 Testosterone: what actually matters#

Because so much marketing targets it:

  • Normal range is wide (roughly 300–1000 ng/dL total T; labs vary). Being at the lower end of normal is not deficiency.
  • Symptoms of genuinely low T: low libido, ED (especially loss of morning erections), fatigue, low mood, reduced muscle mass, increased body fat, reduced body hair, hot flashes, infertility. Without symptoms, a number is not a disease.
  • Causes of low T: obesity (the most common modifiable one), sleep apnea, opioids, anabolic steroid history, testicular injury, pituitary problems, chronic illness, aging.
  • What raises it: losing body fat (if overweight), sleep, resistance training (modest, acute), correcting deficiencies (D, zinc), reducing alcohol, treating sleep apnea. Not masturbation abstinence (Chapter 4). Not supplements (18.5).
  • Testosterone replacement therapy is appropriate for men with symptomatic, confirmed (two morning measurements) low T. It improves libido and energy; its effect on erections alone is modest (ED is usually vascular). It has side effects (infertility, polycythemia, possible cardiovascular considerations under ongoing study) and requires monitoring. Not a lifestyle drug.
  • Masturbation, ejaculation frequency, and testosterone: no meaningful relationship. Chapter 4.

18.8 Stress and mental health — 🟢#

  • Chronic stress raises cortisol, which suppresses the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis (lower T), raises sympathetic tone (worse erections, faster ejaculation), and reduces libido and attention. Stress is a major cause of sexual difficulty in otherwise healthy men.
  • Depression reduces desire, pleasure (anhedonia), and erectile function directly; its treatments often add sexual side effects. Treating depression is the priority; sexual function is negotiated with the prescriber.
  • Anxiety is the primary cause of ED and PE in young men. Performance anxiety specifically is a self-fulfilling loop.
  • Interventions with evidence: exercise (also treats mild-moderate depression and anxiety), sleep, mindfulness/meditation (small-to-moderate effects on sexual function in trials), therapy (CBT for anxiety and depression; sex therapy for sexual anxiety specifically).

18.9 Body image#

Concerns about penis size, body weight, body hair, and general attractiveness impair sexual function through anxiety and self-monitoring — even in solo sex, where there’s no one to impress. Men with size anxiety (nearly all of it unfounded — Chapter 2.8) often report less pleasure and more spectatoring. Interventions: accurate information (Chapter 2), reducing comparison sources (pornography selects for outliers and uses camera tricks), mindfulness (attention on sensation, not on appearance), and — for persistent distress — therapy. Grooming (trimming pubic hair) is a preference, not a requirement; it does make the penis look larger by removing visual obstruction, which is why it’s common.

18.10 The summary hierarchy#

If you want to improve sexual function through lifestyle, in order of impact:

  1. Quit smoking (if you smoke).
  2. Aerobic exercise 150+ minutes/week.
  3. Sleep 7–9 hours; treat apnea.
  4. Lose excess weight, especially abdominal.
  5. Limit alcohol to ≤1 drink before sessions, moderate overall.
  6. Manage stress and treat anxiety/depression.
  7. Pelvic floor training.
  8. Mediterranean-pattern eating.
  9. Review medications with the prescriber.
  10. Everything else (supplements, specific foods, timing) is marginal.

Key takeaways#

  • Erection is cardiovascular. Aerobic exercise, not smoking, sleep, and weight are the levers — and they rival medication in effect size.
  • Sleep produces testosterone and maintains penile tissue via nocturnal erections. Sleep apnea causes ED. Late-night sessions that cut sleep are a bad trade.
  • Diet: Mediterranean pattern and flavonoid-rich foods have evidence. “Semen foods” and “aphrodisiacs” don’t.
  • Supplements: L-citrulline/L-arginine have weak evidence for mild ED; nothing over the counter meaningfully raises T; “enhancement” blends are often adulterated with prescription drugs.
  • Alcohol beyond one drink impairs. Nicotine is the worst common substance for erections. Cannabis at low doses enhances for many; high doses impair. Stimulants produce wanting without function and drive compulsivity. Poppers + ED drugs can be fatal.
  • Medications — SSRIs, beta-blockers, thiazides, antipsychotics, 5-ARIs — commonly impair; alternatives exist for nearly all. Talk to the prescriber.
  • Testosterone: symptoms matter, not just numbers; fat loss, sleep, and treating apnea raise it; masturbation and supplements don’t move it.
  • Stress, anxiety, depression, body image are real sexual function inputs with real treatments.
