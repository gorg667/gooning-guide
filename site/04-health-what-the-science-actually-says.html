Chapter 4 — Health: What the Science Actually Says#

This chapter is the evidence audit. For each commonly claimed benefit, harm, or non-effect of masturbation, we summarize what the research shows, how strong it is, and what a reasonable person should conclude. We err toward understatement: where the data are thin, we say so, even when the conclusion would be flattering to masturbation.

4.1 How to read sexual health research#

A few cautions before the findings:

  • Most of it is self-report. People misremember and misreport sexual behavior. Frequency estimates are especially noisy.
  • Most of it is correlational. “Men who masturbate more have X” does not mean masturbation causes X. Confounders — health, relationship status, libido, testosterone, age, mental health — are everywhere.
  • Publication and funding bias exist in both directions. Some research programs are motivated by religious/moral concern, some by sex-positive advocacy. Read the funding and the framing.
  • Effect sizes matter more than p-values. A “statistically significant” effect that shifts a hormone by 5% for two hours is not clinically meaningful.
  • Sample sizes are often tiny. Many widely cited hormone studies have fewer than 30 participants.

With that said:

4.2 Claimed benefits#

4.2.1 Prostate cancer risk — 🟡 Moderate evidence of association with lower risk#

The Health Professionals Follow-up Study followed ~32,000 men for 18 years. Men who reported ≥21 ejaculations per month had approximately 20% lower risk of being diagnosed with prostate cancer than men reporting 4–7 per month, with the association present for ejaculation frequency at ages 20–29 and 40–49 [Rider 2016]. An earlier Australian case-control study found a similar pattern [Giles 2003].

Caveats. Observational. Men who ejaculate more may be healthier, have more sex, or differ hormonally. Reverse causation is possible (early, undiagnosed prostate disease may reduce sexual activity). The Mayo Clinic’s position is that evidence is “not conclusive.” The mechanistic hypothesis — flushing of carcinogens or reduced crystalloid formation in prostatic fluid — is unproven.

Reasonable conclusion: frequent ejaculation is not harmful to the prostate and may be modestly protective. It is not a cancer-prevention strategy in itself.

4.2.2 Stress reduction and mood — 🟢 Strong for acute effect#

Orgasm produces measurable drops in cortisol and a surge of oxytocin, prolactin, and endorphins. The subjective effects — relaxation, reduced tension, improved mood — are reported consistently across surveys. Masturbation is among the most commonly cited reasons for masturbating in survey research (“to relieve stress,” “to relax”) [Herbenick 2023].

Caveat: acute relief is well-established; effects on baseline mental health are unclear and confounded. Using masturbation as the only stress-management strategy can become problematic (Chapter 15).

4.2.3 Sleep — 🟡 Moderate#

A survey of 778 adults found that orgasm (partnered or solo) before bed was associated with self-reported faster sleep onset and better sleep quality [Lastella 2019]. Mechanistically plausible (prolactin, oxytocin, parasympathetic rebound). Lacks objective polysomnographic confirmation at scale.

4.2.4 Pain relief — 🟠 Weak to moderate#

Orgasm raises pain thresholds substantially in laboratory settings (studied mainly in women, [Whipple & Komisaruk 1985]). Case reports and small studies suggest orgasm can abort migraine or cluster headache in a subset of patients [Hambach 2013] — in that study, about 60% of migraine patients who had sex during an attack reported improvement. Not a reliable treatment; a real effect for some.

4.2.5 Immune function — 🟠 Weak#

One small study (n=11) found a transient increase in circulating leukocytes and natural killer cells after masturbation [Haake 2004]. The effect is short-lived and its health relevance is unknown. Widely over-cited.

4.2.6 Pelvic floor health and erectile function — 🟡 Moderate (indirect)#

Regular erections and pelvic floor use are thought to maintain penile tissue oxygenation and smooth muscle health (“use it or lose it”). After prostatectomy, urologists often prescribe penile rehabilitation — regular erections via medication or vacuum devices — precisely because prolonged absence of erection is associated with fibrosis and shortening. By extension, regular sexual activity in healthy men likely supports long-term erectile function, but direct trials in healthy men are lacking. Sexual activity frequency correlates with lower ED incidence in aging cohorts [Koskimäki 2008 — ~2,000 Finnish men: intercourse <1/week associated with ~2x ED risk vs ≥1/week], but confounding is obvious.

4.2.7 Sexual skill and partnered satisfaction — 🟡 Moderate#

Men who masturbate are more likely to know what they enjoy and communicate it; masturbation is used therapeutically for premature ejaculation, delayed ejaculation, and anorgasmia (Chapter 16). Sex therapists routinely prescribe directed masturbation. Survey data on masturbation and relationship satisfaction is mixed and heavily confounded by relationship quality and desire discrepancy.

4.2.8 Sperm quality — 🟡 Moderate#

Frequent ejaculation (every 1–2 days) is associated with lower DNA fragmentation in sperm compared to long abstinence, even though volume and count per ejaculate decrease. Fertility clinics increasingly recommend short abstinence (1–2 days) before providing a sample [Ayad 2018 review]. Long abstinence (>5–7 days) increases volume but decreases motility and increases oxidative damage.

4.2.9 Self-knowledge, body image, and sexual confidence — 🟢 Consensus#

Not easily quantified, but essentially every sex-education body (WHO, Planned Parenthood, NHS, American Sexual Health Association) frames masturbation as a healthy component of sexual development and self-knowledge.

4.3 Claimed harms#

4.3.1 Testosterone depletion — 🔴 Contradicted#

The claim: masturbation/ejaculation lowers testosterone; abstinence raises it.

The evidence:

  • The most-cited study for abstinence raising T [Jiang 2003] had 28 subjects and reported a testosterone peak of ~145% of baseline on day 7 of abstinence, returning to baseline afterward. The paper has been flagged as retracted in at least one database listing. Even taken at face value, a transient 7-day bump that disappears isn’t a sustained increase.
  • Exton et al. (2001) found slightly higher T after 3 weeks of abstinence in 10 men — small, brief, uncertain.
  • Acute studies during and after masturbation show no meaningful change in T [Isenmann 2021; Krüger 1998]. Small transient rises during arousal itself have been reported (T rises with sexual anticipation), not sustained shifts.
  • Large cross-sectional studies find no relationship between ejaculation frequency and baseline testosterone.

Conclusion: baseline testosterone is governed by the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, sleep, body fat, age, and health. Masturbation is not a meaningful input. The “NoFap testosterone boost” is not supported.

4.3.2 Erectile dysfunction — 🔴 for normal use; 🟡 for specific patterns#

The claim: masturbation (or masturbation to porn) causes ED.

The evidence:

  • Large samples find no consistent association between masturbation frequency or pornography use per se and erectile function [Grubbs & Gola 2019 — three samples; Landripet & Štulhofer 2015 — European samples; Prause & Pfaus 2015 — more porn viewing associated with greater arousal responsiveness].
  • There is a consistent cross-sectional association between self-perceived problematic or compulsive pornography use and ED [Grubbs & Gola 2019; Jacobs 2021 — n≈3,400]. Direction of causation is unknown; anxiety, depression, and moral distress are candidate confounders.
  • Sex therapists document a real clinical pattern where conditioning to a very specific stimulus (a particular grip, speed, or type of visual content) reduces responsiveness to a partner — but this is situational, learned, and reversible [Perelman 2016].

Conclusion: ordinary masturbation does not cause ED. Compulsive or highly idiosyncratic patterns may contribute to situational difficulties in some men. See Chapters 14 and 16.

4.3.3 Hair loss, acne, blindness, weakness, memory loss, back pain — 🔴 Contradicted#

These claims trace to 18th-century texts (Tissot’s L’Onanisme, 1760) and 19th-century moral medicine (Kellogg, Graham). There is no mechanism, no data, and no modern medical body that endorses any of them. Hair loss is genetic and androgen-mediated; masturbation doesn’t change androgens. Acne is hormonal and bacterial; same. Back pain after masturbation, if it occurs, is postural (Chapter 5).

4.3.4 “Semen retention” and vital energy — 🔴 Contradicted#

The idea that semen contains vital essence that is depleted by ejaculation appears in Taoist, Ayurvedic (dhat syndrome), and Victorian traditions. Semen is continuously manufactured; unejaculated sperm are broken down and reabsorbed; there is no finite reservoir and no measurable systemic “depletion.” The nutrients in a typical ejaculate amount to roughly 5–25 calories. Dhat syndrome is recognized as a culture-bound anxiety syndrome, not a physiological condition.

What is real: some men subjectively feel more energetic, focused, or motivated when they abstain for a period. Plausible explanations include reduced prolactin-mediated satiety, redirected attention, placebo/expectation, and for some men, breaking a compulsive pattern that was itself draining. These are real experiences worth respecting; they are not evidence of “vital energy.”

4.3.5 Prostatitis and pelvic pain — 🟡 Complicated#

Chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CPPS / chronic non-bacterial prostatitis) is common and poorly understood. Some men report that frequent masturbation worsens symptoms; others report it helps. Very frequent, forceful ejaculation with a tight, hypertonic pelvic floor may aggravate CPPS. Pelvic floor relaxation training helps many CPPS patients [Anderson 2005]. Reasonable conclusion: if you have pelvic pain, the issue is more likely pelvic floor tension than masturbation itself — see Chapter 10 and a pelvic floor physiotherapist.

4.3.6 Physical injury — 🟢 Real but avoidable#

Friction burns, skin tears, frenulum tears, and (rarely) penile fracture or urethral injury happen. Nearly all are preventable with lubrication, moderate grip, and not bending an erect penis forcefully. Chapter 5 covers this fully.

4.3.7 Desensitization / “death grip” — 🟡 Moderate (clinical)#

Chronic use of very tight grip and/or very fast stroking can lead to a learned requirement for that level of stimulation to reach orgasm — making partnered sex or gentler solo stimulation insufficient. This is Perelman’s “idiosyncratic masturbatory style” [Perelman 2005, 2016], a well-documented contributor to delayed ejaculation. Physical nerve damage is not the mechanism; conditioning is. It is reversible with retraining (Chapter 16).

4.3.8 Compulsivity — 🟢 Real for a minority#

Compulsive Sexual Behaviour Disorder (ICD-11 6C72) is a recognized condition affecting an estimated 3–10% of adults depending on criteria [Kraus 2018; Bőthe 2024]. Masturbation is one of the behaviors most often involved. Critically, the diagnosis excludes distress that stems purely from moral or religious disapproval — frequency alone doesn’t qualify, and neither does guilt. Chapter 15 covers this in depth.

4.3.9 Post-orgasmic illness syndrome (POIS) — 🟢 Real, rare#

A small number of men experience flu-like symptoms, cognitive fog, and fatigue for 2–7 days after every ejaculation [Nguyen 2018 — ~50 cases in literature; likely underreported]. Cause unknown. If this describes you, it’s not psychological — see Chapter 16 and a doctor.

4.4 Frequency: is there a “right” amount?#

No. The research supports no optimal frequency. Points of reference:

  • Survey data shows enormous normal variation: from never to multiple times daily [Herbenick 2010, 2023].
  • Frequency declines with age on average but with wide overlap between age groups.
  • No frequency has been shown to cause harm in the absence of compulsivity or physical symptoms.
  • The ≥21/month associated with lower prostate cancer risk is not a target; it’s a statistical category.
  • Sperm quality is best with ejaculation every 1–3 days if fertility is the goal.

Better questions than “how much”:

  1. Does it interfere with sleep, work, relationships, or responsibilities?
  2. Do I feel unable to stop or cut down when I want to?
  3. Am I doing it to avoid feelings or tasks, more than for pleasure?
  4. Is there pain, irritation, or numbness?
  5. Is it still enjoyable, or has it become mechanical?

If the answers are no, no, no, no, yes — your frequency is fine, whatever the number.

4.5 Abstinence: is there benefit?#

Deliberate abstinence (“semen retention,” “NoFap,” “reboot”) is popular online. The honest evidence summary:

  • No physiological benefit has been demonstrated: not testosterone, not energy, not muscle growth, not cognition.
  • Psychological benefits are reported by many participants and should be taken seriously as experiences — improved mood, motivation, and confidence. Plausible causes: breaking a pattern that had become compulsive, reduced time spent, placebo/expectation, community support, and redirected attention toward goals.
  • Harms are reported too: heightened anxiety around “relapse,” rigid all-or-nothing thinking, shame spirals, and in some communities, misogynistic or pseudoscientific framing.
  • A distinction matters: taking a break from pornography or from compulsive patterns is very different from abstaining from masturbation. Many men find the former beneficial and the latter unnecessary.
  • Recommended framing: periodic breaks (a few days to a couple of weeks) are a useful tool for (a) resetting sensitivity if you’ve been overdoing a specific technique, (b) noticing your patterns, (c) increasing the intensity of the next session. They are not a health intervention.

Key takeaways#

  • The strongest evidence supports masturbation as harmless and mildly beneficial for stress, sleep, and possibly prostate health.
  • The strongest claims against it — testosterone, ED, energy depletion, hair loss — are contradicted.
  • The real risks are specific and avoidable: physical injury (lube, grip), conditioning to a narrow stimulus (variety), and compulsive use (awareness).
  • There is no correct frequency. Judge by interference, control, motive, symptoms, and enjoyment.
  • Abstinence has no demonstrated physiological benefit but can be a useful psychological tool, especially for breaking compulsive or narrow patterns.
