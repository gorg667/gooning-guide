# Chapter 2 — Anatomy: Know Your Equipment

You cannot skillfully play an instrument you've never looked at closely. This chapter maps the male sexual anatomy with an eye toward *function and sensation* — not just what each part is, but what it feels, how densely it's innervated, and how it responds to different kinds of touch. Return to this chapter whenever a later technique mentions a structure you want to locate precisely.

## 2.1 Overview

The male sexual apparatus has four functional groups:

1. **The penis** — the primary organ of sensation and the erectile structure.
2. **The scrotum and testes** — sperm and hormone production; also a sensitive erogenous zone.
3. **The internal glands and ducts** — prostate, seminal vesicles, bulbourethral (Cowper's) glands, vas deferens, epididymis — which produce and transport semen and which, in the case of the prostate, can be a major source of pleasure.
4. **The pelvic floor** — the muscular sling that supports the pelvic organs, controls urination, and produces the rhythmic contractions of orgasm and ejaculation.

Overlaying all of this is the **nervous system**: the pudendal nerve (sensation and voluntary muscle), the pelvic splanchnic nerves (parasympathetic — erection), and the hypogastric nerves (sympathetic — emission and ejaculation). Understanding which nerves do what explains a great deal about technique.

## 2.2 The penis

### 2.2.1 External structure

Working from tip to base:

**Glans (head).** The smooth, mushroom-shaped cap. Highly sensitive, especially in uncircumcised men where it's normally protected by the foreskin and remains moist. The glans is derived from the same embryonic tissue as the clitoris and shares its dense innervation. It contains fewer fine-touch receptors than the frenulum and corona but is extremely responsive to pressure, warmth, and wetness. Overstimulation of the glans can tip into discomfort quickly for many men — this is why "glans-only" techniques often need lubricant and lighter pressure.

**Meatus (urethral opening).** The slit at the tip. Sensitive; some men enjoy very light stimulation around it, others find it uncomfortable. Nothing should be inserted into the urethra without specific knowledge (see Chapter 12 on "sounding" — a practice with real risks).

**Corona (coronal ridge).** The flared rim at the base of the glans. This ridge is packed with nerve endings and is a key sensation zone. Many stroking techniques derive most of their pleasure from the hand or sleeve passing over the corona. The groove behind it is the **coronal sulcus**, where smegma collects in uncircumcised men and where hygiene matters.

**Frenulum.** The small band of tissue on the underside connecting the glans to the shaft (or the foreskin, in uncircumcised men). For most men, this is the single most sensitive spot on the penis — a concentration of fine-touch mechanoreceptors and free nerve endings. The frenulum is often called the "male G-spot" in popular writing (though the prostate also gets this label). Light, focused stimulation of the frenulum with a fingertip, especially lubricated, is among the most reliable ways to raise arousal quickly. In circumcised men the frenulum may be partially or fully removed depending on surgical technique; the remaining tissue and scar line usually retain significant sensitivity.

**Shaft.** The body of the penis. Skin is loose and mobile (it must accommodate erection) and less densely innervated than the glans/frenulum. The shaft's pleasure comes mostly from (a) the skin sliding, which tugs on the frenulum and corona, and (b) pressure on the underlying erectile bodies, which produces a deeper, less sharp sensation. The **dorsal** (top) surface carries the dorsal nerves and vessels; the **ventral** (underside) has the urethra running through the corpus spongiosum and is more sensitive to pressure and light touch along its length, especially the **raphe** — the faint seam line running from the frenulum down the underside to the scrotum and perineum.

**Foreskin (prepuce).** In uncircumcised men, a double-layered fold of skin covering the glans. The inner layer is mucosal (like the inside of your mouth) and contains specialized sensory tissue, particularly at the **ridged band** just inside the tip. The foreskin's gliding motion over the glans is a major mechanism of sensation. Foreskin mobility varies: some men can retract fully when erect, some only partially (see Chapter 5 on phimosis and frenulum breve).

**Circumcised vs. uncircumcised — the sensitivity question.** Research is contested. A careful 2016 study using quantitative sensory testing found no significant difference in penile sensitivity between circumcised and intact men across multiple sites [Bossio 2016]. An earlier study reported reduced fine-touch sensitivity in circumcised men [Sorrells 2007]. Most sexual-medicine reviewers consider the effect, if any, small and highly variable between individuals. What is *not* contested: technique differs. Uncircumcised men have a built-in gliding mechanism and generally need less lube; circumcised men rely more on skin-over-shaft motion and benefit more from lubrication. Both can experience the full range of pleasure.

### 2.2.2 Internal structure

The penis has three cylinders of erectile tissue:

- **Two corpora cavernosa** — paired on the top/sides, these are the main erectile bodies. They're sponge-like chambers surrounded by a tough fibrous sheath (the **tunica albuginea**). During erection they fill with blood and the tunica stretches to its limit, producing rigidity. The tunica is what tears in a **penile fracture** (see Chapter 5).
- **One corpus spongiosum** — on the underside, surrounding the urethra, expanding at the tip into the glans. It stays softer during erection (so the urethra isn't pinched shut during ejaculation). Pressure on the underside of the shaft is pressure on the spongiosum, which many men find pleasurable in a distinct way from stimulating the glans.

**The root and bulb.** About a third of the penis is inside the body. The corpora cavernosa anchor to the pelvic bones (the **crura**), and the corpus spongiosum expands into the **bulb** in the perineum, just in front of the anus. This matters for two reasons: (1) pressure on the perineum stimulates the bulb and root of the penis from the inside — the "external prostate" spot discussed below is actually a mix of bulb, urethra, and prostate-adjacent tissue; and (2) the muscles around the bulb (bulbospongiosus) are what pump semen out during ejaculation and produce the pleasurable pulsing of orgasm.

### 2.2.3 Blood supply and why it matters

Erection is hydraulic: arteries dilate, blood floods the corpora, and the expanding tissue compresses the veins that drain it, trapping blood. This is why:

- Cardiovascular health directly determines erection quality (Chapter 18).
- Constriction rings work by mechanically blocking venous outflow — and why they must not be left on too long (Chapter 13).
- Very tight grip during masturbation can reduce sensation both by compressing nerves and by impeding blood flow.

## 2.3 The scrotum and testes

The **scrotum** is a sac of thin, wrinkled, highly mobile skin. It's rich in nerve endings and temperature-sensitive — it retracts toward the body when cold or aroused (via the cremaster and dartos muscles) and hangs loose when warm. The **raphe** runs down its midline. The scrotal skin is a genuine erogenous zone that most men neglect entirely during solo sex. Light stroking, gentle tugging, cupping and warmth, and stimulation of the raphe all produce distinct sensations that layer onto penile stimulation (Chapter 11).

The **testes** produce sperm and testosterone. They are exquisitely pain-sensitive (the tunica albuginea of the testis is densely innervated) but also respond pleasurably to gentle pressure and rolling. The **epididymis**, a coiled tube on the back of each testis, stores maturing sperm. The **spermatic cord** runs from each testis up into the groin and contains the vas deferens, blood vessels, and nerves; gentle traction on the cord (by pulling the testes down slightly during high arousal) is a classic technique for delaying ejaculation, because testicular elevation is part of the ejaculatory reflex.

**Self-examination.** Testicular cancer is the most common cancer in men aged 15–35 and is highly curable when caught early. Monthly self-exam — rolling each testis gently between thumb and fingers, feeling for hard lumps, changes in size, or persistent aching — is easy to build into a solo session and is discussed in Chapter 5.

## 2.4 The prostate and internal glands

**The prostate** is a walnut-sized gland sitting just below the bladder, wrapped around the urethra. Its **posterior surface** faces the rectum and is separated from it by only a thin wall, which is why it can be palpated (and stimulated) through the anal canal, about 5–7 cm (2–3 inches) inside, toward the front of the body. It produces about 20–30% of seminal fluid — the thin, slightly alkaline component that helps sperm survive. It is surrounded by smooth muscle that contracts during emission, pushing prostatic fluid into the urethra.

**Why it can be pleasurable.** The prostate is richly innervated by the pelvic plexus — the same nerve network that produces the sensations of orgasm. Its posterior surface and the adjacent **seminal vesicles** respond to pressure with a sensation many men describe as "deep," "full," or "like the beginning of an orgasm that keeps going." Prostate-induced orgasms have been described in clinical literature as more intense and more whole-body than penile orgasms, and they may occur without ejaculation, allowing multiple orgasms in one session [Levin 2018]. The mechanism is not well understood — Levin suggests a learning/rewiring process, since many men report no pleasure at first and increasing pleasure with practice. This is discussed at length in Chapter 12.

**The "external prostate" / perineal spot.** Between the scrotum and anus is the **perineum**. Pressing firmly upward here, about midway, compresses the bulb of the penis, the membranous urethra, and, indirectly, the prostate. Many men find rhythmic pressure here during stimulation intensifies orgasm. It's also the spot pressed in the (not recommended) "hold-back" technique to block ejaculation — see Chapter 12 on why that's a bad idea.

**Seminal vesicles.** Two pouches behind the prostate that produce ~60–70% of ejaculate volume: the fructose-rich, viscous fluid. Together with the prostate they explain why the first ejaculate after a long gap is larger — the vesicles fill up. They are also part of what's felt during deep prostate stimulation.

**Bulbourethral (Cowper's) glands.** Two small glands below the prostate that produce **pre-ejaculate** ("pre-cum") — the clear fluid that appears during arousal. It neutralizes residual urine acidity in the urethra and lubricates. Volume varies enormously between men (from none visible to several milliliters) and this variation is normal. Pre-ejaculate can contain sperm, which matters for partnered sex, not solo.

## 2.5 The pelvic floor

The **pelvic floor** is a hammock of muscles spanning the bottom of the pelvis from pubic bone to tailbone. The relevant muscles for sexual function are:

- **Bulbospongiosus** — wraps around the bulb of the penis. Contracts rhythmically during ejaculation to expel semen. Also what you squeeze to push out the last drops of urine. Strong, well-coordinated bulbospongiosus contractions are associated with more forceful ejaculation and more intense orgasm sensation.
- **Ischiocavernosus** — runs along the crura (roots) of the penis. Contracting it compresses the roots, momentarily increasing rigidity — it's what makes an erection "bounce."
- **Pubococcygeus (PC muscle)** and the broader **levator ani** group — the deeper sling supporting bladder and rectum. The PC muscle is what most "Kegel" instructions refer to. It's involved in the urge-to-ejaculate sensation and can be trained for control.
- **External urethral sphincter** and **external anal sphincter** — voluntary sphincters; contracting the pelvic floor contracts these too.

The pelvic floor is the closest thing to a **control panel** for male sexual response that's under voluntary control. Its tone affects erection firmness, ejaculatory timing, and orgasm intensity. It is also a place where men store tension — a chronically tight (hypertonic) pelvic floor is associated with premature ejaculation, pelvic pain, and even erectile problems. Chapter 10 is dedicated to training it in both directions: strengthening and relaxing.

**Finding it.** Sit or lie down. Imagine stopping urine mid-stream, or lifting your testicles slightly, or squeezing as if to stop passing gas. That lift/squeeze is your pelvic floor. When erect, the same contraction makes the penis twitch upward. Now try to release completely — let the area drop and soften. That release is the "reverse Kegel."

## 2.6 The nervous system of male sexual response

Three systems interlock:

**Somatic (pudendal nerve).** Carries sensation from the penis, scrotum, and perineum to the spinal cord and brain, and motor commands to the pelvic floor muscles. This is the "touch" pathway. Pudendal nerve compression (from cycling, prolonged sitting on hard surfaces, or very tight grip) can cause numbness — usually reversible.

**Parasympathetic (pelvic splanchnic nerves, S2–S4).** The "rest and digest" system. Triggers erection by releasing nitric oxide, which relaxes the smooth muscle of the corpora and lets blood flood in. This is why anxiety (a sympathetic state) kills erections and relaxation supports them. It is also why many erection problems respond to breathing, mindfulness, and reducing performance pressure (Chapters 8, 16).

**Sympathetic (hypogastric nerves, T10–L2).** The "fight or flight" system. Paradoxically, it's what drives **emission** — the movement of semen into the urethra — and is involved in triggering ejaculation. This creates the well-known pattern: high sympathetic arousal (anxiety, excitement) tends toward *faster* ejaculation and *weaker* erection, while relaxed parasympathetic dominance supports firmer erection and slower ejaculation. Arousal control (Chapter 9) is largely learning to keep the sympathetic system from tipping over prematurely.

**Central processing.** The brain integrates all of this. Sensory input from the penis reaches the somatosensory cortex; reward circuits (nucleus accumbens, ventral tegmental area) drive wanting; the hypothalamus coordinates hormonal release; and at orgasm, imaging studies show widespread activation with notable deactivation of parts of the prefrontal cortex — the neural correlate of "losing yourself" [Georgiadis 2006]. The **brain is the largest sex organ** is not just a slogan: attention, expectation, and mental state modulate genital sensation profoundly, which is the basis of the mindfulness material in Chapter 8 and much of Chapter 11.

## 2.7 Erogenous zones beyond the genitals

Skin everywhere has sensory receptors, but certain areas are especially responsive, and most men leave them untouched during solo sex:

- **Nipples.** Densely innervated. Roughly half of men report nipple stimulation increases arousal [Levin & Meston 2006]. Sensitivity varies enormously; some men have almost none, others can approach orgasm from nipple stimulation alone with practice.
- **Inner thighs, lower abdomen, groin crease.** Light touch here (the "tease zone") raises anticipation.
- **Perineum.** Covered above.
- **Anus and surrounding skin.** Extremely nerve-dense (the same pudendal nerve). External stimulation alone is pleasurable for many men regardless of sexual orientation; internal stimulation reaches the prostate.
- **Neck, ears, scalp, lower back, buttocks.** Secondary zones that contribute to whole-body arousal.
- **Feet, hands, lips.** Highly innervated; role in arousal is more individual.

Chapter 11 covers mapping your own erogenous zones systematically.

## 2.8 Size, shape, and normal variation

Because anxiety about size is nearly universal and undermines pleasure, the facts:

- A systematic review of over 15,000 men found average erect length ≈ 13.1 cm (5.2 in) and average erect circumference ≈ 11.7 cm (4.6 in) [Veale 2015]. Flaccid size varies far more than erect size and predicts erect size poorly ("growers vs. showers").
- Curvature of up to about 30 degrees in any direction is normal and present in a majority of men. Curvature that is new, painful, progressive, or associated with a palpable plaque may be Peyronie's disease and warrants a urologist (Chapter 5).
- The glans may be larger or smaller than the shaft; the shaft may taper or widen; veins may be prominent; skin tone differs from the rest of the body; small bumps around the corona (pearly penile papules) and small white/yellow dots on the shaft (Fordyce spots) are normal anatomy, not disease.
- One testis usually hangs lower (typically the left) and one is often slightly larger. Both are normal.

Perception of one's own size is skewed by viewing angle (looking down foreshortens), comparison to pornography (which selects for outliers and uses camera angles), and locker-room comparison of flaccid sizes. Size has weak correlation with the sensations available to *you* — nerve density, not length, determines what you feel.

---

### Key takeaways

- The **frenulum, corona, and glans** are the most nerve-dense external zones; the **shaft** provides deeper, pressure-based sensation; the **underside** is more sensitive than the top.
- Uncircumcised and circumcised men differ in *technique* (gliding vs. sliding, lube needs) more than in *capacity* for pleasure.
- A third of the penis is internal; the **perineum** gives access to the bulb and root.
- The **prostate** is reachable 5–7 cm inside the rectum toward the front, is richly innervated, and can produce distinct, often non-ejaculatory orgasms with practice.
- The **pelvic floor** (bulbospongiosus, ischiocavernosus, PC) is the voluntary control panel for erection firmness, ejaculatory timing, and orgasm intensity.
- **Parasympathetic** = erection (relaxation); **sympathetic** = ejaculation (excitement). Managing this balance is the core of control.
- The **scrotum, nipples, perineum, inner thighs, and anus** are underused erogenous zones.
- Size and shape variation is wide and normal; sensation depends on nerves, not centimeters.
