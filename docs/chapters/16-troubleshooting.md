# Chapter 16 — Troubleshooting: Common Problems and Fixes

This is the clinical chapter. Each section covers a problem: what it is, what causes it, what you can do yourself, and when to see a professional. The self-help here is drawn from sex therapy practice and the research literature; it isn't a substitute for a clinician when one is needed, and each section says when that is.

## 16.1 Premature ejaculation (PE)

**Definition.** The International Society for Sexual Medicine defines lifelong PE as ejaculation that always or nearly always occurs within about one minute of vaginal penetration, from the first sexual experiences; acquired PE as a clinically significant and bothersome reduction in latency (often to ≤3 minutes) in men who previously had normal control. Both require inability to delay and negative consequences (distress, avoidance) [ISSM 2014]. For solo purposes, the relevant feature is: **you ejaculate sooner than you want to, and can't reliably delay it.**

**Prevalence.** Self-reported PE affects 20–30% of men; strict-criteria lifelong PE is 2–5%.

**Causes.** Lifelong PE likely has a neurobiological component (serotonin receptor sensitivity, penile hypersensitivity, and genetic factors are all implicated). Acquired PE is associated with ED (rushing before losing the erection), anxiety, hyperthyroidism, prostatitis, and relationship factors. In both, a **hypertonic pelvic floor**, **anxiety/sympathetic dominance**, and **learned rushing** (from adolescent masturbation habits of finishing fast to avoid discovery) are common contributors and are the modifiable targets.

**Self-help (evidence-based):**

1. **Stop-start and squeeze** (Chapter 9) — the original behavioral treatments [Semans 1956; Masters & Johnson 1970]. Practice solo with a lubricated hand, then a warm sleeve, then with thrusting motion. 8–12 weeks.
2. **Pelvic floor training with emphasis on release** (Chapter 10) — 82.5% of men with lifelong PE gained control after 12 weeks in Pastore's study [2014]. The reverse Kegel at 8.5–9 is the key skill.
3. **Breath** — slow, exhale-emphasized breathing throughout; a long exhale at the edge (Chapter 8).
4. **Body relaxation at high arousal** — thighs, buttocks, jaw, hands (Chapter 8.8).
5. **Arousal awareness** — learn the 1–10 scale until you can name your level in real time.
6. **Slow down the whole session** — men with PE often masturbate fast and hard, training the reflex. Two weeks of slow, light, lubricated sessions alone often produces measurable change.
7. **Masturbate before partnered sex** — 1–2 hours prior; the second arousal cycle is slower for most men. A legitimate, widely used tactic.
8. **Reduce anxiety** — PE is self-reinforcing through performance anxiety. Mindfulness practice, and taking the pressure off ("we'll have sex; if I come fast we'll continue other ways") help.

**Medical options** (via a doctor): daily or on-demand SSRIs (paroxetine, sertraline, dapoxetine where available) — well-evidenced; topical anesthetics (lidocaine/prilocaine sprays or creams) — effective, use with a condom or wash off to avoid numbing a partner; PDE5 inhibitors if ED coexists; tramadol (off-label, less preferred). Combining medication with behavioral training often works better than either alone [Perelman 2006].

**See a doctor if:** self-help hasn't helped after 3 months; PE is acquired and sudden (check thyroid, prostate); ED is also present; distress is significant.

## 16.2 Delayed ejaculation (DE) and anorgasmia

**Definition.** Marked delay in, infrequency of, or absence of ejaculation/orgasm despite adequate stimulation and desire, causing distress. "Adequate" is subjective; men with DE commonly report needing 30+ minutes of intense stimulation, or being unable to finish with a partner (but able alone), or unable to finish at all.

**Prevalence.** 1–4% in most surveys; more common than assumed and rising in clinical caseloads.

**Causes.**

- **Idiosyncratic masturbatory style** [Perelman 2005, 2016] — the most common contributor in men who can orgasm alone but not with a partner. A technique (speed, pressure, grip, prone rubbing, specific position) that no partner can replicate; high frequency (Perelman: >3x/week correlates); and a gap between the fantasy required and the partner present.
- **Medications** — SSRIs are the most common cause of acquired DE; also antipsychotics, some blood pressure medications, opioids, alcohol.
- **Neurological** — diabetes, MS, spinal injury, pelvic surgery.
- **Hormonal** — low testosterone, hyperprolactinemia, hypothyroidism (uncommon but checkable).
- **Psychological** — self-monitoring/spectatoring, anxiety, fear of pregnancy or of "letting go," relationship issues, religious guilt about ejaculation.
- **Age** — latency increases with age normally; DE is more common over 50.
- **Reduced penile sensitivity** — with age, diabetes, or heavy pressure conditioning.

**Self-help:**

1. **Masturbation retraining** (Perelman's approach):
   - **Identify** your idiosyncratic pattern honestly. Speed? Pressure? Position? Dry? Prone? Specific fantasy?
   - **Suspend** the idiosyncratic method entirely for 2–6 weeks. Not necessarily suspend masturbation — suspend the *method*.
   - **Substitute** stimulation that resembles what a partner could provide: lubricated, moderate pressure, moderate speed, supine, enveloping (a warm sleeve is ideal). Initially this may not get you to orgasm. That's expected. Stay with it; arousal adapts to the available stimulation within a few weeks in most men.
   - **Reduce frequency** temporarily if it's high (Perelman recommends this). Every 3–4 days rather than daily, so arousal accumulates.
   - **Align fantasy** — include content resembling your actual or plausible partnered sex.
   - **Gradually reintroduce** variety once orgasm with "partner-like" stimulation is reliable.
2. **Reduce spectatoring.** DE men are often watching themselves, evaluating, worrying about how long it's taking. Mindfulness (Chapter 8) — attention on sensation, not on progress — is directly therapeutic. Remove the clock. Remove the goal for several sessions.
3. **Increase arousal inputs.** Many DE men plateau at 7 and never learn what gets them to 8–9. Systematic exploration (Chapter 6 inventory, Chapter 11 mapping) — nipples, perineum, prostate, vibration, fantasy variety — often reveals unused inputs. Vibration in particular helps many men with DE because it recruits receptors that hands don't.
4. **Pelvic floor.** Both strengthening (weak expulsion) and release (a tight floor can inhibit the reflex).
5. **Medication review.** If you're on an SSRI or similar, talk to the prescriber — dose adjustment, drug switch (bupropion, mirtazapine have less sexual side effect), or timing strategies exist.
6. **Reduce alcohol.**

**Medical options:** no approved drug for DE. Off-label: cabergoline (dopamine agonist), bupropion, oxytocin (experimental), amantadine; testosterone if low. Vibratory stimulation devices (medical-grade) are used clinically. A sex therapist is the main resource.

**See a doctor if:** DE is new and unexplained (check meds, hormones, glucose); there's no ejaculation at all (anejaculation — may be retrograde ejaculation or neurological); it's causing significant distress or fertility issues.

## 16.3 "Death grip" and reduced sensitivity

Covered in Chapters 5 and 6; consolidated here.

**Presentation.** Needing very firm pressure and/or fast speed to feel much or to reach orgasm; reduced sensation with lighter touch or with a partner; sometimes visible skin changes (thickening, redness at the grip zone).

**Cause.** Learned threshold (conditioning), not nerve damage. Possibly some transient skin/nerve contribution from chronic compression, which resolves.

**Fix — the 4-week recalibration:**

- **Week 1:** all sessions lubricated. Grip at half your habitual pressure. Speed at half. It will feel like less. Orgasm may be hard. Don't compensate by going harder; if you don't finish, stop without finishing.
- **Week 2:** same, plus one session with the non-dominant hand only, and one with a sleeve (moderate, enveloping pressure).
- **Week 3:** grip at a quarter of your old pressure. Add mindful attention (Chapter 8). Add a secondary zone. Notice the sensation returning.
- **Week 4:** experiment with the barely-touching level. Most men find by now that light, lubricated stimulation is producing more sensation than the death grip ever did.
- **Ongoing:** rotate techniques; never let one grip/speed become the only path.

If you also have DE, this is the same program as 16.2's retraining. If sensation doesn't return in 6–8 weeks, or if there's numbness rather than just reduced sensation, see a doctor (check for diabetes, pudendal nerve issues, medications).

## 16.4 Erectile difficulties

**Definitions.** Erectile dysfunction is the persistent inability to attain or maintain an erection sufficient for satisfactory sexual performance. Occasional difficulty is universal and not ED. Solo-relevant presentations: difficulty getting hard alone; getting hard alone but not with a partner (situational); losing erection during long sessions; softer erections than before.

**First, the normal stuff:**

- **Erections fluctuate during long sessions.** Softening during pauses or low-stimulation phases is normal. It comes back.
- **Mindful/slow sessions** produce less rigid erections than fast, high-stimulation ones. Normal.
- **Refractory period** — you won't get hard soon after ejaculating. Normal. Duration increases with age.
- **Tired, drunk, stressed, cold, distracted** — all produce weaker erections. Normal.
- **Morning erections** present → the plumbing works; difficulties are more likely psychological/situational.

**Causes of real ED:**

- **Vascular** — the most common cause over 40. ED is an early warning sign of cardiovascular disease (the penile arteries are small and show atherosclerosis before the coronary arteries). New ED in a man over 40 warrants a cardiovascular check.
- **Metabolic** — diabetes, obesity, metabolic syndrome.
- **Hormonal** — low testosterone (less common than assumed as a sole cause), thyroid, high prolactin.
- **Neurological** — diabetes, MS, pelvic surgery, spinal issues.
- **Medications** — antidepressants, antihypertensives (especially beta-blockers, thiazides), antipsychotics, 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors (finasteride), antihistamines, opioids.
- **Substances** — alcohol, tobacco (major), cannabis (heavy), stimulants.
- **Psychological** — performance anxiety (the dominant cause in men under 40), depression, stress, relationship issues, guilt.
- **Situational conditioning** — reliably hard with porn/specific stimulus, not otherwise (Chapter 14).
- **Pelvic floor hypertonicity** — can compress arteries and nerves (Chapter 10).
- **Sleep** — sleep apnea is strongly associated with ED.

**Self-help:**

1. **Rule out the normal** (above).
2. **Cardiovascular health** — exercise, weight, blood pressure, smoking cessation. This is the single most effective ED intervention for men over 40 and takes months. Aerobic exercise alone improves erectile function measurably [Gerbild 2018 meta-analysis].
3. **Pelvic floor training** — 40% cured, 35% improved in Dorey's RCT [2005].
4. **Reduce performance pressure.** For situational/psychogenic ED: sessions with no goal, mindful, in which erection is not required and not monitored. The paradox of erection: trying to get one prevents it. Sensate focus without erection as the goal is the sex-therapy standard.
5. **Screen-free arousal** if you suspect conditioning. A 2–6 week break from pornography with fantasy-based masturbation (Chapter 14).
6. **Sleep, alcohol, tobacco.**
7. **Medication review** with the prescriber.
8. **PDE5 inhibitors** (sildenafil, tadalafil) — highly effective, safe for most men, available by prescription (and over the counter in some countries). Using them for a period can break the anxiety cycle in psychogenic ED by restoring confidence. They don't create arousal; they amplify the response to arousal that's present. Not with nitrates; caution with poppers.

**See a doctor if:** ED is persistent (>3 months), especially if new and you're over 40 (cardiovascular workup); morning erections have disappeared; there's pain, curvature, or a lump; you're on medications that may contribute; it's causing significant distress.

## 16.5 Pain

**During or after ejaculation (dysorgasmia / painful ejaculation).** Causes: prostatitis/CPPS (most common), pelvic floor hypertonicity, urethritis (STI or not), seminal vesicle inflammation, post-surgical, some medications (certain antidepressants), psychological. **Do:** pelvic floor release (Chapter 10.5), warm baths, hydration; if persistent >2 weeks or with fever/discharge — doctor (urine test, prostate exam).

**Testicular ache after prolonged arousal ("blue balls" / epididymal hypertension).** Real, benign, from vasocongestion. Resolves with ejaculation or in a few hours. Not dangerous. Severe, sudden, or persistent testicular pain is different — torsion (emergency), epididymitis (infection, doctor), or trauma.

**Pain in the penis with erection.** New curvature + pain + possibly a lump = Peyronie's; see a urologist early. Pain at the frenulum = tear or frenulum breve. Pain in the shaft without curvature after a session = probably friction/bruising; rest.

**Skin pain/rawness.** Friction. Chapter 5.

**Pelvic/perineal pain, chronic.** Chronic pelvic pain syndrome. Often pelvic floor tension. Pelvic floor physiotherapy has the best evidence [Anderson 2005]. Urologist to rule out other causes.

**Pain with anal play.** Stop. Insufficient lubrication, insufficient relaxation, too large, too fast, hemorrhoid or fissure. Persistent pain after — doctor.

**Lower back pain after masturbation.** Posture and muscle tension (arching, bracing). Stretch, change position, relax the body at high arousal.

**Headache at orgasm.** "Coital cephalalgia" — sudden severe headache at or near orgasm. Usually benign (a form of exertional headache) but the first occurrence should be evaluated because the same presentation can indicate a hemorrhage. Recurrent benign cases are managed with medication (indomethacin, beta-blockers) if needed.

## 16.6 Blood in semen (hematospermia)

Alarming, almost always benign, especially under 40. Causes: minor vessel rupture from vigorous sex/masturbation, prostatitis, urethritis, recent prostate procedure, hypertension, and rarely, more serious conditions (more relevant over 40 with recurrent episodes). **Do:** a single episode that resolves — note it; recurrent episodes, over 40, or with other symptoms (pain, urinary symptoms, weight loss) — see a doctor.

## 16.7 Retrograde ejaculation

Orgasm with little or no semen, and cloudy urine afterward (semen went into the bladder). Causes: alpha-blockers (tamsulosin and others, for prostate or blood pressure), some antidepressants, diabetes (nerve damage), prostate/bladder-neck surgery, and — deliberately — the perineal "hold-back" or strong "clench" (Chapter 12). Harmless in itself except for fertility. If unexpected and persistent, see a doctor (medication review, glucose check).

## 16.8 Post-orgasmic illness syndrome (POIS)

Flu-like symptoms — fatigue, muscle aches, fever/chills sensation, cognitive fog, irritability, congestion — beginning within minutes to hours after ejaculation and lasting 2–7 days. Rare; ~50 published cases but likely underreported [Nguyen 2018]. Cause unknown; hypotheses include an allergic/autoimmune reaction to seminal components and neuroendocrine dysregulation. Diagnosis is clinical (the pattern). Treatment is trial-and-error: antihistamines, NSAIDs, SSRIs, hyposensitization to autologous semen (experimental), and others [Ponce 2025 systematic review]. If this is you, it's not psychological, and a urologist or immunologist with an interest in it is the resource. Non-ejaculatory orgasm (Chapter 12) may help some POIS patients, suggestively implicating ejaculation specifically.

## 16.9 Post-coital dysphoria (post-orgasm sadness)

Feelings of sadness, tearfulness, irritability, anxiety, or emptiness after orgasm, without an obvious reason. Reported by ~40% of men at some point and ~4% regularly [Maczkowiack & Schweitzer 2019]. Cause unclear — possibly the hormonal shift (prolactin, drop in dopamine), possibly psychological (shame, unresolved feelings surfacing when defenses drop). Usually brief. **Do:** don't rush to fix it; stay in the afterglow; let the feeling be there; it typically passes in minutes. If persistent, frequent, and distressing, or if it's tied to shame about the behavior, a therapist can help.

## 16.10 Loss of interest / low libido

Reduced desire to masturbate or for sex generally. Causes: depression (very common), stress, fatigue, sleep problems, low testosterone (check if other symptoms — fatigue, low mood, reduced morning erections, reduced muscle), medications (SSRIs, antiandrogens, opioids), relationship issues, hypothyroidism, chronic illness, alcohol, aging (gradual normal decline), and *habituation to a narrow pattern* — the same routine has stopped being interesting. **Do:** address sleep, stress, exercise, alcohol; vary the practice (Chapters 6, 11); if persistent with other symptoms, a doctor for a hormonal and mood assessment. Low desire without distress is not a disorder.

## 16.11 Numbness and reduced sensation

Distinguish: **transient numbness** after vibrator use or a very long session (normal; resolves in minutes to hours), **reduced sensitivity** from conditioning (16.3), **age-related decline** (gradual, normal, compensated with more varied and intense stimulation), and **true neuropathy** (persistent, often with tingling, may affect the perineum/thighs too; causes include diabetes, pudendal nerve compression from cycling or prolonged sitting, spinal issues, B12 deficiency, alcohol). Persistent numbness → doctor.

## 16.12 Erection won't go down (priapism)

An erection lasting more than 4 hours without stimulation is a medical emergency (ischemic priapism damages erectile tissue permanently). Causes: sickle cell, certain medications (trazodone, some antipsychotics, ED drugs — especially injected ones), cocaine, spinal injury. **Emergency room. Do not wait.**

## 16.13 Curvature

Congenital curvature (present since puberty, stable, painless, <30°) is normal. New curvature, especially with pain, a palpable plaque, indentation, or shortening, suggests Peyronie's disease. Early urological assessment matters; treatments are more effective in the acute (first 12 months) phase.

## 16.14 Semen changes

Volume decrease: normal with frequent ejaculation, dehydration, age, alpha-blockers, and after long sessions (multiple emissions). Yellow: normal after abstinence; possibly urine mix. Clumpy/jelly-like: normal (coagulation before liquefaction). Watery/clear: normal with very frequent ejaculation; persistently very watery — semen analysis if fertility is a concern. Foul odor: possible infection. Blood: 16.6. Green/grey with pain or discharge: infection — doctor.

## 16.15 Lumps and bumps

**Normal:** pearly penile papules (small, uniform, flesh-colored dots in rows around the corona), Fordyce spots (small white/yellow dots on the shaft or scrotum — sebaceous glands), angiokeratomas on the scrotum (small dark red/purple dots), hair follicles, sebaceous cysts (small, mobile, sometimes with a central pore), lymphocele after vigorous sex (cord-like swelling behind the corona, resolves), varicocele ("bag of worms" in the scrotum), epididymis (soft structure on the back of each testis).

**See a doctor:** any lump *in* the testis (hard, painless); warts (cauliflower texture — HPV); ulcers or sores (syphilis, herpes, others); a growing, changing, or bleeding lesion; a plaque in the shaft (Peyronie's); molluscum (pearly dome-shaped bumps with a central dimple — contagious, benign, treatable).

---

### Key takeaways

- **PE**: stop-start + pelvic floor release + breath + slowing down + arousal awareness; 8–12 weeks; SSRIs/topicals if needed.
- **DE**: identify and suspend the idiosyncratic method; substitute partner-like stimulation; reduce frequency temporarily; stop spectatoring; add inputs (vibration, prostate, zones); review medications.
- **Death grip**: 4-week recalibration to lighter, lubricated, slower, varied. Learned, not damage.
- **ED**: rule out normal fluctuation; cardiovascular health; pelvic floor; remove performance pressure; screen break if conditioned; PDE5i to break the anxiety cycle; new ED over 40 = heart check.
- **Pain**: pelvic floor release for most ejaculatory/pelvic pain; blue balls are benign; new curvature = urologist early.
- **Blood in semen** is almost always benign; check if recurrent or over 40.
- **POIS** is real and rare; not psychological.
- **Emergencies**: priapism >4 h, fracture, torsion, paraphimosis, stuck objects, first thunderclap orgasm headache.
