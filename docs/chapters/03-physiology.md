# Chapter 3 — Physiology: Arousal, Erection, Orgasm, Ejaculation

This chapter explains what happens in your body from the first flicker of interest to the post-orgasm afterglow. Everything in later chapters — edging, multiple orgasms, pelvic-floor control, pleasure maximization — is an application of the mechanics described here.

## 3.1 Models of the sexual response cycle

**Masters & Johnson (1966)** described four phases from laboratory observation of hundreds of subjects:

1. **Excitement** — arousal begins; erection starts; heart rate and blood pressure rise; scrotum tightens; nipples may harden.
2. **Plateau** — arousal is high and sustained; erection full; glans darkens and swells slightly; testes elevate and enlarge (up to 50%); pre-ejaculate appears; breathing quickens; muscle tension (myotonia) builds throughout the body.
3. **Orgasm** — a few seconds of intense sensation with rhythmic contractions of the pelvic floor (roughly 0.8-second intervals), usually accompanied by ejaculation.
4. **Resolution** — return to baseline. In men, followed by a **refractory period** during which further orgasm is difficult or impossible.

**Kaplan (1979)** added **desire** as a preceding phase, recognizing that arousal doesn't always start from the body. **Basson (2000)** proposed a circular model (originally for women, but applicable to anyone) where arousal can precede desire — you start touching without being especially "in the mood," and desire follows. This is highly relevant to solo practice: many of the best sessions begin with low desire and slow arousal, rather than urgent horniness.

**The arousal scale.** For practical purposes we'll use a 1–10 scale throughout this guide:

- **1–2**: no arousal, flaccid, not thinking about sex.
- **3–4**: interest, early erection, pleasant but easily interrupted.
- **5–6**: firm erection, clearly aroused, could stop without frustration.
- **7**: strongly aroused, stopping would be mildly frustrating; the body starts to "want" to continue.
- **8**: building toward orgasm, breathing changes, pelvic floor starting to tense involuntarily.
- **9**: the edge. Orgasm is imminent if stimulation continues for a few more seconds. Involuntary pelvic pulses may begin.
- **9.5**: the point of no return (PONR) — emission has started; orgasm will occur even if all stimulation stops.
- **10**: orgasm.

Learning to identify where you are on this scale, in real time, is the foundation of every control technique.

## 3.2 Erection: the hydraulics

**Trigger.** Erection can begin from three sources:

- **Psychogenic** — thoughts, fantasy, visual input. Signals travel from the brain down the spinal cord.
- **Reflexogenic** — direct touch to the genitals, processed at the spinal level (S2–S4) with minimal brain involvement. This is why men with certain spinal cord injuries can still get erections from touch, and why erections happen at inconvenient times from friction alone.
- **Nocturnal** — 3–5 erections per night during REM sleep, lasting 25–35 minutes each. Morning erections are the last of these. They're a useful health indicator: if you reliably wake with erections, the plumbing is fine and any daytime difficulties are more likely psychological.

**Mechanism.** Parasympathetic nerves release **nitric oxide (NO)** into the smooth muscle of the corpora cavernosa. NO triggers production of **cGMP**, which relaxes the smooth muscle. Relaxed muscle means the arteries feeding the corpora open wide (arterial inflow increases many-fold), the sponge-like sinusoids fill with blood, and the expanding tissue presses the draining veins flat against the tunica albuginea (**veno-occlusion**). Inflow up, outflow blocked: the penis fills and stiffens.

**Ending.** The enzyme **PDE5** breaks down cGMP; smooth muscle contracts; arteries narrow; veins reopen; blood drains. PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil) work by blocking this enzyme, keeping cGMP around longer. They do not create arousal — they amplify the erection response to arousal that's already there.

**Why anxiety kills erections.** The sympathetic nervous system releases norepinephrine, which contracts the smooth muscle of the corpora — the exact opposite of NO's effect. Fear, performance pressure, distraction, cold, and stimulants (caffeine excess, nicotine, cocaine, amphetamines) all push sympathetic. The practical lesson: **relaxation is a prerequisite, not a luxury**. Breath, warmth, privacy, and freedom from time pressure are physiological inputs.

**Erection quality fluctuates normally.** During a long session, erection typically waxes and wanes — firm at high arousal, softer during pauses or when attention wanders. This is not dysfunction. Men who are used to short, urgent sessions sometimes panic the first time an erection softens during a 30-minute edging session. It comes back.

## 3.3 Arousal: what's happening beyond the penis

As arousal climbs through plateau, the body changes systemically:

- **Heart rate** rises to 100–175 bpm; **blood pressure** rises; **breathing** deepens and quickens.
- **Myotonia** — muscle tension — builds involuntarily, especially in the thighs, buttocks, abdomen, hands, and feet. Some of this is useful (pelvic floor engagement), some counterproductive (jaw clenching, shallow breathing, hunched shoulders). Learning to release unnecessary tension while allowing pelvic tension is a core skill (Chapter 11).
- **Sex flush** — reddening of the chest, neck, face in some men.
- **Testicular elevation.** The cremaster muscles pull the testes toward the body. Full elevation is a reliable sign of approaching orgasm. Gently pulling them back down is a classic delay technique.
- **Glans engorgement** and color deepening; **pre-ejaculate** secretion.
- **Attention narrows.** At high arousal, the brain's attentional field shrinks toward the sensation — the beginning of the prefrontal deactivation seen at orgasm.

**Neurochemistry of arousal.** The dominant player is **dopamine**, released in the mesolimbic reward pathway. Crucially, dopamine is the neurotransmitter of *anticipation and wanting*, not primarily of pleasure itself [Berridge & Kringelbach 2015]. This distinction underlies a great deal of practical technique: the "wanting" state of high arousal, teased and extended, is itself intensely pleasurable, and it is dopamine-driven. Pleasure in the consummatory sense — the "liking" — is more associated with **endogenous opioids** and **endocannabinoids** in the brain's hedonic hotspots. Serotonin, by contrast, is generally *inhibitory* to orgasm — which is why SSRIs delay or block ejaculation and are used off-label for premature ejaculation.

## 3.4 The point of no return and ejaculation

Ejaculation is a spinal reflex with two phases, coordinated by a group of neurons in the lumbar spinal cord sometimes called the **spinal ejaculation generator**.

**Phase 1 — Emission.** Under sympathetic control (T10–L2). The smooth muscle of the epididymis, vas deferens, seminal vesicles, and prostate contract in sequence, moving sperm and seminal fluids into the **prostatic urethra**. The **internal urethral sphincter** (bladder neck) closes tightly so semen goes forward, not into the bladder. This is the "point of no return" — once emission has begun, the sensation of inevitability is unmistakable, and expulsion will follow in 1–3 seconds regardless of what you do.

**Phase 2 — Expulsion.** Under somatic control (pudendal nerve, S2–S4). The **bulbospongiosus** and **ischiocavernosus** muscles, plus the pelvic floor, contract rhythmically — typically 10–15 contractions, the first 3–5 strong at ~0.8 second intervals, then weaker and more irregular. Each contraction propels semen along the urethra. The external urethral sphincter relaxes to let it through.

**Volume and composition.** Typical ejaculate is 1.5–5 mL; ~65% seminal vesicle fluid, ~25–30% prostatic fluid, ~5% sperm and testicular fluid, ~1% bulbourethral. Volume increases with abstinence up to about 3–5 days, then plateaus. Volume is *not* an indicator of health, virility, or pleasure. It decreases with age, with frequent ejaculation, with dehydration, and with certain medications (notably alpha-blockers and some antidepressants).

**Force and distance** depend on pelvic floor muscle strength and coordination, ejaculate volume, and urethral resistance. A strong pelvic floor (Chapter 10) noticeably increases force — which many men find increases the pleasure of orgasm as well.

## 3.5 Orgasm vs. ejaculation: they are not the same thing

This is one of the most important conceptual points in the entire guide.

**Ejaculation** is the physical expulsion of semen — a spinal reflex.

**Orgasm** is the brain event: the peak of pleasure, the altered state, the sense of release and the involuntary contractions felt throughout the body.

They *usually* happen together in men, and so most men experience them as a single thing. But they are separable:

- **Ejaculation without orgasm** occurs with some spinal injuries, with certain drugs, and occasionally in "anhedonic ejaculation" — semen comes out, but there's no peak, little pleasure. It also happens partially in "ruined orgasms" when stimulation stops exactly at the PONR.
- **Orgasm without ejaculation** occurs in prepubertal boys (who can orgasm before they produce semen), in men after prostatectomy, in men who have learned to inhibit ejaculation at orgasm (Chapter 12), in many prostate-induced orgasms, and in the so-called "dry orgasms" that men can learn to produce with pelvic floor control and edging practice.

The **refractory period** appears to be tied primarily to *ejaculation*, not orgasm. Men who achieve non-ejaculatory orgasms frequently report little or no refractory period and can have several orgasms in one session [Levin 2018; anecdotal reports abound]. This is the physiological basis of male multiple orgasm.

## 3.6 What happens in the brain at orgasm

PET and fMRI studies of men during ejaculation/orgasm [Georgiadis 2006; Holstege 2003] show:

- Strong activation of the **ventral tegmental area** — the same dopamine hub that lights up on heroin. The reward is real and powerful.
- Activation of the **cerebellum** (muscle coordination), **thalamus**, and **anterior cingulate**.
- Marked **deactivation of the prefrontal cortex**, particularly the left orbitofrontal area — associated with self-monitoring, judgment, and inhibition. This is the neural substrate of "letting go." Anxiety, self-consciousness, and judgment (all prefrontal activities) interfere with orgasm; the harder you try, the harder it gets. Men with delayed ejaculation often show excessive self-monitoring.
- **Amygdala** deactivation — reduced fear and vigilance. You cannot orgasm while your brain is scanning for threats, which is another reason privacy and safety are prerequisites.

**Hormonal cascade at orgasm:**

- **Oxytocin** spikes at orgasm — associated with the feeling of warmth, bonding, and satiety. Also contributes to the smooth muscle contractions of emission.
- **Prolactin** surges immediately after orgasm and stays elevated for about an hour. Prolactin is inhibitory to dopamine and sexual arousal, and has been proposed as the main driver of the refractory period and post-orgasm satiety [Krüger 2003]. Intriguingly, the prolactin surge after intercourse was measured to be roughly 400% larger than after masturbation in one study [Brody & Krüger 2006], which may explain why partnered orgasm often feels more satiating — though this study is small and the interpretation is debated. The prolactin hypothesis of the refractory period is itself contested [Turley & Rowland 2013].
- **Endorphins and endocannabinoids** — the "afterglow": relaxation, pain reduction, mild euphoria, sleepiness.
- **Vasopressin** — also released; role less clear.
- **Cortisol** typically drops after orgasm — a measurable stress-reduction effect.

## 3.7 The refractory period

After ejaculation, most men enter a period during which the penis is insensitive or hypersensitive-in-an-unpleasant-way, erection subsides or won't return, and further stimulation feels irritating or produces nothing. Duration:

- Teens and early 20s: often a few minutes to 20 minutes.
- 30s–40s: 30 minutes to a few hours is typical.
- 50s and beyond: several hours to a day or more is common.
- Huge individual variation at every age.

**What determines it?** Not fully understood. Prolactin is one candidate (see above). Serotonin release, a "resetting" of the spinal ejaculation generator, and peripheral factors (sympathetic tone in the penis) have all been proposed. What's practically relevant:

- The refractory period is **not a fixed physiological wall**. It varies with arousal level, novelty of stimulation, and whether ejaculation occurred.
- **Non-ejaculatory orgasms** produce little or no refractory period in most men who can achieve them (Chapter 12).
- Some men can achieve a **second ejaculatory orgasm** within minutes with continued or renewed stimulation — the second is often less intense, with smaller volume. This is more common in younger men and with strong arousal.
- The refractory period **lengthens** with fatigue, alcohol, recent ejaculation, and age; **shortens** with novelty, strong arousal, and (anecdotally) with regular practice of edging and pelvic floor training.

## 3.8 The role of the mind: attention and expectation

Physiology is not just plumbing. The same touch can feel like nothing or like everything depending on where attention is:

- **Attention amplifies sensation.** Neural signals from the penis are processed in the somatosensory cortex; attention modulates the gain on that processing. Distracted masturbation (scrolling, half-watching a video, thinking about work) produces measurably less sensation than the same touch with focused attention. This is the basis of mindful masturbation (Chapter 8).
- **Expectation shapes experience.** Believing an orgasm will be intense makes it more likely to be. Believing you'll finish too fast makes that more likely too. Anxious expectation is self-fulfilling through sympathetic activation.
- **Habituation is real.** Any stimulus repeated identically loses its punch — the nervous system is built to detect change. This applies to touch technique, fantasy content, and visual stimuli. Variety isn't a luxury; it's a neurological requirement for sustained pleasure (Chapter 11).
- **Sensory gating at high arousal.** As arousal climbs, the brain gates out competing input — pain thresholds rise, sounds fade, the field narrows. At 8–9 on the arousal scale, you are in a mildly altered state. This gating can be *deepened* deliberately (breath, sound, eyes closed, rhythmic movement) to make the experience more immersive.

## 3.9 Common physiological variations (all normal)

- **Ejaculating when soft or semi-erect.** Full erection is not required for orgasm/ejaculation.
- **Orgasm intensity varies day to day.** Sleep, stress, time since last orgasm, arousal build-up duration, and attention all affect it.
- **Dribbling vs. shooting.** Depends on pelvic floor strength, volume, urethral resistance. Both normal.
- **Post-ejaculation sensitivity.** Many men find the glans intensely, unpleasantly sensitive for a few minutes after. Normal.
- **Post-orgasm sadness or flatness (post-coital dysphoria).** Reported by a meaningful minority of men; usually brief. Not a sign of anything wrong unless persistent and distressing [Maczkowiack & Schweitzer 2019].
- **Semen color and consistency.** Ranges from clear to white to slightly yellowish; thick to thin; can be clumpy or gel-like initially and liquefy over 15–30 minutes. Yellow tinge after long abstinence is normal. Brown, red, or pink (blood) warrants a look — usually benign (Chapter 16) but worth checking.
- **Pain-free contractions in the perineum or anus during orgasm** — normal; that's the pelvic floor.
- **Erection during sleep and on waking**, in non-sexual contexts, from vibration, from needing to urinate — all normal.

---

### Key takeaways

- Arousal moves through excitement → plateau → orgasm → resolution; learn to place yourself on a **1–10 scale** with **9 = edge** and **9.5 = point of no return**.
- **Erection is parasympathetic (relaxation); ejaculation is sympathetic (excitement).** Anxiety weakens erection and speeds ejaculation.
- **Emission** (semen enters the urethra — the PONR) precedes **expulsion** (rhythmic pelvic floor contractions) by 1–3 seconds.
- **Orgasm and ejaculation are separate events** that usually coincide. The refractory period is tied mainly to ejaculation. Separating them is the basis of multiple orgasms.
- **Dopamine = wanting/anticipation; opioids = liking/consummation.** Extended anticipation is itself a huge pleasure source.
- **Prolactin** rises after orgasm and probably drives satiety and the refractory period (contested).
- The **prefrontal cortex shuts down** at orgasm — self-monitoring and judgment prevent it. Letting go is physiological.
- **Attention amplifies sensation; habituation dulls it.** Focus and variety are non-negotiable for maximal pleasure.
